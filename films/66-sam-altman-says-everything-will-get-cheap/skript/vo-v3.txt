Housing. Education. Food. Clothing. Everything necessary.
His reason: artificial intelligence and robots will do the work.
Your receipts say the opposite. Rent, up. Groceries, up. The power bill, up.
And your paycheck has to cover all of it.
Yet he could still be right. Prices don't fall all at once. They fall one after another.
So what gets cheap first, and which of your bills never will?
The first price has already fallen. Almost to nothing.

But one thing on his list may never get cheap. It may even rise.

This January, he made the promise again.

Deflationary pressure. In plain words: prices heading down.
His argument is stronger than it sounds: almost every price you pay is mostly other people's time.
Of every dollar Americans spend on food, more than half pays for somebody's work. The farm gets about twelve cents.
Let superintelligence do the thinking and robots the lifting, and the price sinks toward what the machines cost.
He even pictures a house, built by robots, for about the cost of renting them.
Then again, he runs the company behind ChatGPT. Cheap intelligence is what he sells.
But his claim can be tested.
Six price tags, from pure thought to solid ground. Then a seventh, with your name on it.
And for each, one question: how much of this price is work?

Start where a price is nothing but work. Not lifting. Thinking.
Knowledge and advice.
That programmer's week for less than a dollar is Altman's own example. And by his count, AI gets about ten times cheaper every year.
Across Europe, about three in ten literary translators say it has already cost them work.
But a falling cost is not yet a falling price.
Law firms have AI too. Yet the vast majority of legal fees in America are still billed by the hour. Many firms even raised their rates.
So that's what gets cheap first: pure thinking. But only as fast as competition forces it.
Which explains a strange case: something became nearly free, and its price kept climbing.

Education. It's on his list.
In some studies, students with a well-built AI tutor learn more than they do in class. And the tutor costs almost nothing.
So a degree should be getting cheaper. Its price tag isn't. This fall, the sticker price at America's public universities went up again.
Because a big part of that price was never the knowledge. It's the certificate. And only the university can print it.
So the second tag splits in two. The knowledge is nearly free. The diploma is not.

So the third tag does fall. Slowly, and not in a straight line.
Because a thing is never only work. It's also materials, chips and power. And power sits under every tag.

Energy.
Altman says where the falling stops: once machines build the data centers, intelligence should cost little more than its electricity.
That makes energy the floor. And the floor isn't falling. In five years, American energy prices rose by about a third.
Unless AI can lower the floor itself. Demis Hassabis, the Nobel laureate who runs Google DeepMind, thinks it could help crack clean energy. He has a name for the world after that.

Radical abundance. It's a forecast, from a man whose company builds AI. But if it comes true, every other tag falls further.
For now, the fourth tag stays up.

One tag needs energy, machines, and something no factory can make.
Food.
Remember the food dollar: more than half is work. Machines can take much of that out.
But the ground under the food just set a record. American cropland has never cost more.
So the fifth tag can fall only part of the way: the work comes out of the price, the ground stays in.
Which leaves the tag with the most ground in it.

Housing. The first word on his list.
Go back to his robot-built house. Listen to how that sentence begins: if robots can build a house on land you already own.
Even in his own picture, the land is already paid for.
And in America, about forty cents of every dollar a home is worth isn't the house. It's the ground under it.
Robots can make walls cheap. They can't make more ground.
Altman never hid this. He wrote it himself: the price of limited resources like land may rise even more dramatically.
So the sixth tag stays hard. It may even rise.
That makes Altman half right. Machines can take the work out of a price. They can't take out what's scarce. And he knows which half is which.

So what does that do to your money? This isn't financial advice. It's the seventh price tag. The one with your name on it.
Run the same test. How much of this price is work? All of it.
It's your wage.
Think back to the first price that fell: a programmer's week, for less than a dollar. That wasn't only a bargain. It was a paycheck.
By his own argument, things get cheap because work gets cheap. And work is what most of us sell.
This summer, American businesses produced more with almost the same hours of work. And the share of that income going to workers fell to the lowest level on record.
That's no proof AI did it. But recently, the Nobel-winning economist Daron Acemoglu pointed to exactly this number.

Altman saw it too. His promise has a second part. In full: everything necessary will be cheap, and everyone will have enough money to be able to afford it.
And if the rules don't adapt? In the same essay, he wrote: most people will end up worse off than they are today.
Even for him, cheap was never enough.

So how does everyone get enough money? Three answers are on the table. Each has a real weakness.
The first is the market: let prices fall, and every dollar buys more. It worked for your phone. Its weakness is the seventh tag: wages can fall right along with prices.
The second is a fund: everyone owns a share. That was Altman's plan, paid for by exactly two things: big companies, and land.
Alaska already pays its people a yearly check from its oil wealth: twelve hundred dollars this year. His plan imagined more than ten times that.
Its weakness: a fund pays what politics allows. Alaska's lawmakers now set the amount every year.
The third is a check: the government sends everyone money. Altman backed a test of it: a thousand dollars a month, for three years. People bought basics, and worked a little less.
Its weakness is the bill.
Even Altman has changed his own answer more than once.

Your wage is a price too.
