# AI Will End Capitalism · Entwurf A (Hook-Ansatz A: Kontrast-Zahl)
Off-Text, nur gesprochener Text · 1.505 Wörter · ~10:20 Min · danach ~15 s ruhiges Schlussbild ohne Sprecher

## Station 1 · The event (0:00–0:31)

AI will end capitalism. This spring, America's biggest companies kept more of every sales dollar as profit than in any quarter since tracking began. In the same three months, working people got the smallest share of what American business produced since nineteen forty-seven. Two records. Same quarter. Opposite directions. So is it true? And if it is, how does capitalism actually end? Not with a crash – with a disappearing act. So what disappears first?

## Station 2 · The circle (0:31–1:42)

Mo Gawdat, formerly of Google X, in his own words: "The very base of capitalism, which is labor arbitrage – to hire you for a dollar and then sell what you make for two – is going to disappear." Barclays is cutting around five thousand jobs this year and says AI is part of the reason. Its quarterly profit just rose twelve percent. Follow that dollar. It's your paycheck. You spend it: groceries, the mortgage, the truck payment. Your spending is somebody's sales. Their sales become profit, and profit becomes next month's paychecks. That circle is the economy. Two thirds of everything America makes is bought by people like you, with money that mostly comes from work. Put a machine inside the circle that works but never buys, and the circle has a gap in it. Hire for a dollar. Then hire a machine for pennies. So is that how it ends? The jobs go, the buying stops, the circle breaks? That's the version everyone tells. It's true. And it's the small part.

## Station 3 · The twist (1:42–3:08)

Look at the layoffs. In October, American employers announced the fewest job cuts of any October in three years. AI was named as the reason in about one in five cuts this year. The jobs are mostly still here. So if the jobs aren't what's vanishing, what is? [pause] Prices. Hire for a dollar, sell for two: that only works while what you make is scarce. Capitalism doesn't run on jobs. It runs on scarcity. Only what's scarce has a price, and only what has a price makes a profit. And for three hundred thousand years, there was exactly one way to get new thinking: a human being, who had to be fed, housed, and paid. Every price you've ever paid had that inside it. In late twenty twenty-two, a batch of AI answers cost about twenty dollars. Two years later, the same batch cost seven cents. Epoch AI, which tracks this, says the same capability now gets about thirteen times cheaper every year. Paul Mason saw the shape of it more than a decade ago: "markets are based on scarcity while information is abundant." Thinking is turning into information. Copied, not hired. So what happens to an economy when the thing it runs on stops having a price?

## Station 4 · What becomes free (3:08–4:48)

We've watched prices hit zero before. The encyclopedia your parents paid off in installments: in nineteen ninety, Britannica sold a hundred and twenty thousand sets. Then the price of a fact fell to nothing. In twenty twelve, after two hundred and forty-four years, the presses stopped. Wikipedia does the job today for about a hundred and ninety million dollars a year, free to you. Music: when the price of a song hit zero, the business shrank by more than half, and only grew back as a subscription. Keep that in mind. Now the same thing is reaching thinking. First words and code. Then advice, diagnosis, design. Then everything built out of those. Chegg sold homework help to students. Since twenty twenty-one, it has lost ninety-nine percent of its value. Last fall it cut almost half its staff and named AI as the reason. The letter from your lawyer. The logo for your brother-in-law's roofing company. Price by price, toward zero. And the value doesn't leave with the price. Researchers once asked people what they'd need to be paid to give up search engines, which cost them nothing. The typical answer: about seventeen thousand dollars a year. The price vanished. The value stayed. So if thinking is free, where does the money go? One question before we follow it: if this is changing how you read the headlines, would you subscribe? It's how these films reach the next person who needs them.

## Station 5 · What stays scarce (4:48–7:45)

Remember the music business. It didn't die. It found a new scarce thing to sell. Capitalism always does; it moves to whatever is still scarce. Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, wrote last year that "the cost of intelligence should eventually converge to near the cost of electricity." If thinking costs what the power costs, then what you're paying for is the power. And the chips. And the ground the data center stands on. Nvidia, which makes the chips, took in ninety-six billion dollars in a single quarter this summer. Ninety-three percent of it came from data centers. Its gross margin: seventy-five cents on every dollar. Loudoun County, Virginia, holds the biggest cluster of data centers on earth. The county says land for one now sells for two to six million dollars an acre. Three companies hold almost two thirds of the world's cloud. It's also the ground under your own house. Across the rich world, about eighty percent of the rise in home prices since the Second World War wasn't the house. It was the land. Land. Power. Chips. Time. Trust. The things no model can copy. That's where the money pools when everything else goes to zero. Now look at the first picture again: record profit, record low for work. Nobody can prove AI did that yet. But that's the direction. And what it leaves behind isn't capitalism as you've known it. The dollar-for-two game shrinks. What grows in its place is a toll on whatever can't be copied. So the real risk was never that AI takes your job. It's that everything you need becomes nearly free, except what can't be copied – and that ends up in very few hands. In that future you don't go hungry. You pay the toll. Forever, to whoever holds the ground, the power, and the chips. [pause] That isn't a law of nature. It's a set of rules. In nineteen oh four, a woman named Lizzie Magie patented a board game to teach exactly that. You know it as Monopoly. She called it The Landlord's Game, and built it on one idea from the economist Henry George: the ground is valuable because of everyone around it. So whoever holds it owes the rest of us, and work shouldn't be taxed. Her game had two sets of rules. Under one, a single player ends up owning everything. Under the other, everybody wins. We kept the first. So what would the second look like now – a world where almost everything is abundant, and the few things that stay scarce are shared, fairly?

## Station 6 · What comes after (7:45–9:43)

It's simpler than it sounds, and parts of it already run. What becomes abundant becomes free, for everyone. The United States built GPS for its military, then switched it on for the whole world at no charge. By twenty seventeen, American companies alone had gained about one point four trillion dollars from it. Put it out there free, and everyone earns on it. That's the rule for whatever AI makes abundant. And what stays scarce gets managed together. Land: since last year, the German state of Baden-Württemberg taxes property on the value of the land alone, not the building. Build on it, and your tax doesn't rise. Germany's top tax court upheld it this April. The tax moves off your work and off what you build, onto what nobody made. Housing: since twenty nineteen, when Vienna zones new land for homes, usually two thirds of it has to be affordable apartments. And the land price is capped by law. Whether that's enough, Vienna still argues about. Energy: off Copenhagen, a wind farm has turned since the year two thousand. Half belongs to the city's utility. The other half to about eight and a half thousand ordinary people who bought in through a cooperative. The wind over that water pays the people who live beside it. And the data centers? That page is still mostly blank. Who owns the thinking machines – a county, a cooperative, three companies – is being decided right now, in county zoning meetings and state tax codes. None of this takes a dollar from anyone's account. It decides, up front, where the value of the scarce things flows: to the few who hold them, or to everyone who makes them valuable.

## Station 7 · Closing (9:43–10:22)

Capitalism won't end with a bang. It ends quietly, price by price. I think that's the best news in this whole story: the end of capitalism isn't something to survive, it's something to design. The question was never whether it ends, but what we put in its place – and whether you're in the room when that's decided. So tell me below: what's the first price you ever watched fall to zero?
