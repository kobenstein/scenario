## Station 1 · The event (0:00–0:34)

AI will end capitalism. This year, April to June: America's biggest companies kept more of every sales dollar as profit than at any point since the count began in two thousand nine. In the same three months, people who work for a living got their smallest share of what American business produced since nineteen forty-seven. Two records. Same quarter. Opposite directions. Nobody can prove AI did that. Yet. So how does capitalism end? Not with a crash. With a disappearing act. What disappears first?

## Station 2 · The circle (0:34–1:23)

Mo Gawdat, formerly of Google X, says the base of capitalism is going to disappear: "to hire you for a dollar and then sell what you make for two." Follow that dollar. It's your paycheck. You spend it: groceries, the mortgage, the truck payment. Your spending is somebody's sales. Their profit is next month's paychecks. That circle is the economy.

ON SCREEN (Zitatkarte, belegter Wortlaut, kein Clip): Mo Gawdat, formerly Google X · Business Insider, Sept. 2025 · "The very base of capitalism, which is labor arbitrage – to hire you for a dollar and then sell what you make for two – is going to disappear."

Now put a machine in the circle that works for pennies and never buys. Barclays did it this year: five thousand jobs gone, AI named as one reason, profit up twelve percent. Is that how it ends: the jobs go, the buying stops, the circle breaks? That's the version everyone tells. It's true. And it's the small part.

## Station 3 · The twist (1:23–2:55)

Because AI doesn't end capitalism by taking your job. It ends it by taking the price off the thing you were paid to do. Look at the layoffs. This October, American employers announced the fewest job cuts of any October in three years. The jobs are mostly still here. What's vanishing is the price.

Hire for a dollar, sell for two: that only works while what you make is scarce. Capitalism doesn't run on jobs. It runs on scarcity. Only what's scarce has a price. Nobody sends you a bill for air. And only what has a price makes a profit. Until now, new thinking had exactly one source: a human being, who had to be fed, housed and paid. The lawyer's hour. The doctor's. The engineer's. Every price you've ever paid had that inside it.

In late twenty twenty-two, AI at the level of the first ChatGPT cost about twenty dollars per million tokens, the unit it's sold by. Two years later: seven cents. And it keeps falling, about thirteen times cheaper every year, says Epoch AI. Paul Mason saw the shape of it in twenty fifteen: "markets are based on scarcity while information is abundant." Now information means the thinking itself. Copied, not hired. What happens to an economy when the thing it runs on stops having a price?

## Station 4 · What becomes free (2:55–4:55)

We've watched a price hit zero before. And each time, the same question: who still gets paid? In nineteen ninety, Britannica sold a hundred and twenty thousand sets of encyclopedias. Then the price of a fact fell to nothing. In twenty twelve, after two hundred and forty-four years, the presses stopped. Wikipedia runs on about a hundred and ninety million dollars a year, free to you. Music: when the price of a song hit zero, the business shrank by more than half, and only grew back as a subscription. The price didn't die. It moved: off the song, onto the door.

Now the same thing is reaching thinking. First words and code: a draft, a summary, a bug fix, for cents. Then advice: the second opinion on your scan, the lease your landlord sent. Then everything built out of thinking: the drug, the bridge, the lesson. Chegg sold homework help to students. Since twenty twenty-one, it has lost ninety-nine percent of its value. A year ago it cut almost half its staff and named AI as the reason. Canva made design cheap enough for anyone, and this month reported its first growth dip in ten years. Canva says AI isn't the reason. The price of a design is falling either way. The people who built those products didn't fail. The price left.

And the value? In twenty nineteen, researchers asked people what it would take to give up search engines, which cost them nothing. The typical answer: seventeen thousand dollars a year. The price vanished. The value stayed.

Value with no price goes looking for a door. Music found one. Capitalism doesn't vanish. It retreats, behind the doors. If you want to follow it there, would you subscribe? [pause]

Watch where it goes.

## Station 5 · What stays scarce (4:55–7:28)

The doors are the things that can't be copied. The ground. The power. The chips. Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, wrote last year that "the cost of intelligence should eventually converge to near the cost of electricity." Read it from the other side: what's left on the bill is the power. The chips. The ground under the data center.

Nvidia, which makes the chips, took in ninety-six billion dollars in a single quarter this summer. It kept three quarters of every dollar as gross profit. That isn't villainy. That's what scarcity pays. Loudoun County, Virginia, holds the biggest cluster of data centers on earth. The county says land for one now sells for two to six million dollars an acre.

The oldest door is the one you pay for every month. Across fourteen rich countries, about eighty percent of the rise in house prices since the Second World War came from one thing: the land underneath. Not the bricks. The dirt.

Now look at the first picture again: record profit, record low for work. That's the direction. Markets are doing exactly what they're built to do: follow the price.

[softly] Not a crash, then. A world where almost everything is nearly free, and the few things that aren't belong to very few people. In that world you don't go hungry. You pay at the door. Every month, to whoever holds the ground, the power and the chips. I don't think the real story of this decade is the machine. It's the ground the machine stands on. [pause]

That isn't a law of nature. It's a set of rules. In nineteen oh four, Lizzie Magie patented a board game to teach exactly that. You know it as Monopoly. She called it The Landlord's Game. It was built on one idea from the economist Henry George: land is valuable because of everyone around it. So what it earns belongs to everyone. Her game had two sets of rules. Under one, a single player ends up owning everything. Under the other, everybody wins. We kept the first. The second got forgotten.

What would it look like now: a world where almost everything is abundant, and the few things that stay scarce are shared, fairly?

## Station 6 · What comes after (7:28–9:36)

The second set of rules is already in play, quietly, in a few places. What becomes abundant is set free, for everyone. The United States built GPS and switched it on for the whole world, free. By twenty seventeen, American companies alone had gained about one point four trillion dollars from it. Put it out there free, and everyone earns on it. That's the rule for whatever AI makes abundant: a free layer, like roads, paid for together instead of at a door, one by one. For a kid in Lagos and a plumber in Ohio alike.

And what stays scarce, we run together. Land first, because nobody made it. Since last year, the German state of Baden-Württemberg taxes property on the value of the land alone, not the building. Build on it, and your tax doesn't rise. Germany's top tax court upheld it this April. Tax what nobody made, not what people build.

Housing: since twenty nineteen, when Vienna zones new land for homes, two thirds of it usually has to be affordable apartments. And the land price is capped by law. Whether that's enough, Vienna still argues about.

Energy: off Copenhagen, a wind farm has turned since the year two thousand. Half belongs to the city's utility. The other half to about eight and a half thousand residents, through a cooperative. The wind was always free. The door to it, they chose to share. [pause]

Now ask the same question about the data center going up on that field in Virginia. Who owns the door? That's being decided right now, in state legislatures and county zoning meetings, where a data center can't vote. You can.

None of this takes what anyone already owns. It decides, up front, where the value of the scarce things flows: to the few who hold them, or to everyone who makes them valuable.

## Station 7 · Closing (9:36–10:31)

Capitalism won't end with a crash. It goes the way the encyclopedia went: one price at a time, while the value stays. Whether it ends there, or just moves in behind the doors: same board, two sets of rules. I think that's the best news in this story: the end of capitalism isn't something to survive. It's something to design.

The question was never whether it ends. It's what we put in its place, and whether you're in the room when that's decided. Tell me below: which price in your line of work falls next? And if you think the end of capitalism should be a good ending, subscribe. That's what this channel is for. [pause]

The thinking is about to be free. The ground it stands on is still ours to decide. Your move.
