# AI Will End Capitalism · Entwurf C (Hook-Ansatz C: Szene im Präsens mit „you“)

## Station 1 · Hook: Das Ereignis (0:00–0:35)

AI will end capitalism. You can watch it start. Right now, sixty thousand people at Microsoft hold an offer: money, if they leave. Two days after that offer went out, the company reported thirty-six billion dollars of quarterly profit. Mo Gawdat, formerly of Google X: "The very base of capitalism, which is labor arbitrage, to hire you for a dollar and then sell what you make for two, is going to disappear." But it won't end with a crash. It ends one price at a time. [pause] How? And what takes its place?

## Station 2 · Der Kreislauf (0:35–1:30)

Start with your paycheck. Between April and June this year, workers got the smallest share of what American businesses produced since records began in nineteen forty-seven. Capitalism is a loop: wages become purchases, purchases become profits, profits become next month's wages. An AI works inside that loop and buys nothing. No rent, no groceries, no truck. With every task it takes over, a slice of the dollar leaves wages. It goes to profit. Or, when a competitor undercuts, into a lower price. [pause] So is that the ending? Not yet. Last month, American companies announced fewer job cuts than in any October in three years. Microsoft says AI isn't replacing the people it cuts. What's shrinking isn't the jobs. It's the share. And that is only the visible part.

## Station 3 · Die Wendung: Preise gegen null (1:30–2:35)

Because AI doesn't end capitalism by taking your job. It ends it by taking the price off the thing you were paid to do. Capitalism doesn't run on jobs. It runs on scarcity. Only what is scarce has a price, and only what has a price makes a profit. The writer Paul Mason put it in one line back in twenty fifteen: markets are based on scarcity, while information is abundant. And what AI is making abundant is the thing you've always paid the most for, by the hour: a skilled mind. The lawyer's, the doctor's, the engineer's. Now follow the lower price all the way down. The AI that could write your emails in late twenty twenty-two cost about twenty dollars per million tokens. Two years later, the same ability cost seven cents. And the same capability keeps getting about thirteen times cheaper every year, according to Epoch AI. [pause] So what happens to an economy when its most valuable good costs almost nothing?

## Station 4 · Was gratis wird (2:35–4:35)

It's already happening, in rings. The first ring: answers, text, code. Chegg sold homework help to students by subscription. Then a free AI answered the same questions. Since twenty twenty-one, Chegg has lost more than ninety-nine percent of its market value. Last fall it cut almost half its staff, citing the new realities of AI. The next ring is yours: the second opinion on your scan, the lease your landlord sent, the plans for the extension on your house. Each of those used to cost you an afternoon and a few hundred dollars. Each is sliding toward free. And the ring after that is everything built out of thinking: the drug, the bridge, the lesson. We have watched a price fall to zero before, just slower. Britannica sold one hundred twenty thousand sets of encyclopedias in nineteen ninety. By the end, eight thousand. In twenty twelve, after two hundred forty-four years, it stopped printing. Not because people stopped wanting to know things. Because Wikipedia gave it away, and all of Wikipedia runs on about one hundred ninety million dollars a year. The price went to zero. The value didn't. In one experiment, people asked for about seventeen thousand dollars a year to give up search engines. Search engines that cost them nothing. [pause] So who still earns, when the thing itself is free? Music answers that. After nineteen ninety-nine, as copying went free, the American music business lost more than half its revenue, and only grew again as a subscription. The price didn't die. It moved: off the song, onto the door. Watch where the doors go. If you'd like to keep following this, one film at a time, would you subscribe?

## Station 5 · Was knapp bleibt (4:35–7:45)

Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, wrote last year that the cost of intelligence should eventually converge to near the cost of electricity. Read that from the other side. Electricity isn't free. It's a door. When thinking costs almost nothing, the money doesn't vanish. It flows to whatever is still scarce: the power line, the chip, the land under the building that holds the chips. Loudoun County, Virginia, is the largest cluster of data centers on Earth. There, the county says, an acre of land for a data center now costs two to six million dollars. Three companies, Amazon, Microsoft and Google, run sixty-three percent of the world's cloud. Three companies collect more than half of all the advertising money outside China. And the oldest door of all is the one you pay for every month. Across fourteen rich countries, about eighty percent of the rise in house prices since the Second World War came from one thing. The price of the land underneath. Not the house. The ground. From your kitchen table it looks like this. The second opinion is free, the plans are free, and the lot you'd build on costs more every year. This is not a villain story. Nobody at Microsoft is being cruel. The loop is doing exactly what it was built to do: send money toward whatever is scarce. For as long as anyone can remember, the scarce thing was skilled human time. That's what a wage buys. Now the scarce things are land, energy, chips, and the minerals inside them. Plus two that never show up on a balance sheet: your time, and whom you trust. An economist will tell you this isn't an ending at all, just capitalism moving house. Fair enough. But look at where it's moving to. [softly] This is the sober version of the danger. Not machines in the streets. A world where almost everything is nearly free, and the few things that aren't belong to very few people. A toll road across an open field. I don't think the real story of this decade is the machine. It's the ground the machine stands on. Which leaves the only question that matters. Can we build a world where almost everything is plentiful, and the little that stays scarce is shared fairly? Nineteen oh four. Lizzie Magie patents The Landlord's Game. You've played it: little houses, rent, one winner holding everything. She built it as a lesson about Henry George's idea, that the value of land belongs to everyone. So it came with two sets of rules. Under the first, one player ends up owning the ground, and everyone else pays rent until they're broke. Under the second, the value of the land is shared, and everybody wins. The first set became Monopoly. The second set got forgotten.

## Station 6 · Was danach kommt (7:45–9:25)

The second set has two halves, and both already run somewhere. What becomes abundant is set free, for everyone. The United States runs GPS and charges no one. American businesses alone drew about one point four trillion dollars of value from it by twenty seventeen. Put the abundant thing out free, and everyone earns on it. Wikipedia is the same move. The doctor's second opinion and the architect's plan can go the same way: free, for everyone, like GPS. And what stays scarce is held in common, so what it earns goes to all of us. Not by taking anything away. By changing what we tax, and who owns the door. Since last year, one German state taxes property on the value of the land alone, not on what's built on it. So an empty lot in the middle of town stops paying. Germany's highest tax court upheld it this spring. Vienna, since twenty nineteen, sends most newly zoned housing land to affordable homes, with the land price for that part capped. Whether that's enough, Vienna still argues about. Off Copenhagen, a wind farm has been turning since the year two thousand. Half of it belongs to the city's utility. The other half to about eight and a half thousand residents, through a cooperative. The wind was always free. The door to it, they chose to share. [pause] Now ask the same question about the data center going up on that field in Virginia. Who owns the door? That's still being decided, in state legislatures and city councils, where a data center can't vote. You can.

## Station 7 · Schluss (9:25–10:10)

So capitalism won't end with a crash. It ends the way the encyclopedia did: quietly, one price at a time, while the value stays. The question was never whether. It's what we put in its place: a toll road, or the second set of rules. Nothing has to be taken from anyone. You just have to show up where the rules for land, power and chips get written. Which price in your line of work falls next? Tell me below. And if you think the end of capitalism should be a good ending, subscribe. That's what this channel is for. [pause] The thinking is about to be free. The ground it stands on is still ours to decide.
