# AI Will End Capitalism — Entwurf B (Hook: Preis-Schock)

## Station 1 · Hook (0:00–0:31)
AI will end capitalism. Not by taking your job. The same AI ability now gets about thirteen times cheaper every year. Not a forecast — a measurement. Same answers, same code, same designs, for a fraction of last year's price. That's the price of thinking, heading for zero. And capitalism is a machine that runs on prices. So what happens to a machine that runs on prices, when the most valuable thing it sells stops having one?

## Station 2 · The loop (0:31–1:26)
Between April and June this year, America's biggest companies kept more of every dollar of sales as profit than at any time since tracking began. In those same months, working people got the smallest share of what American business produced since records began. You'd expect the first sign to be layoffs. It isn't. This October, American companies announced the fewest layoffs of any October in years. The jobs are still here. The money is already moving. Because capitalism is a loop: your wages become someone's sales, their sales become profits, profits become wages again. Now put a machine into that loop that works but never buys groceries, never pays rent, never gets a haircut. The money never leaves the building. Is that how it ends? No. That's only the part you can see.

## Station 3 · The turn (1:26–3:01)
That's how AI ends capitalism. Not with a crash. By draining the price out of the one thing everything else is made of: thinking. Because underneath the jobs and the loop, capitalism runs on scarcity. Only what's scarce has a price. Only what has a price makes a profit. Only what makes a profit gets built. Prices are how the machine sees — and the moment something becomes as plentiful as air, it goes blind to it. Nobody sends you a bill for air. The AI that answered the first chatbot questions in late twenty twenty-two? Two years later it cost more than two hundred and eighty times less to run. Same ability. The journalist Paul Mason wrote it down back in twenty fifteen: "markets are based on scarcity while information is abundant." Then, information meant songs and articles. Now it means the thinking itself: the plan, the diagnosis, the design, the code, the advice. For most of human history the scarce thing was food. Then land. Then your hours. Whoever held the scarce thing held the power. And thinking was the scarcest of all: it lived in a few heads and took decades to grow. Now it pours out of warehouses full of chips. So the question isn't whether machines take jobs. It's what an economy does when its main ingredient costs nothing. And what, exactly, goes free first?

## Station 4 · What becomes free (3:01–5:01)
Start with what's already free. Text and code went first: a draft, a summary, a bug fix, for cents. Advice is next: the tax question, the contract clause, the second opinion on a scan. Then designs: the floor plan, the logo, the molecule. Then everything built from those: the app, the course, the business plan. When thinking is free, the things made of thinking get cheap. You've seen this before. Britannica was the business of knowing things for two hundred and forty-four years. In twenty twelve it stopped printing. Wikipedia now does that job for about a hundred and ninety million dollars a year — not sold, just there, like air. Music lost half its revenue when copying became free, and came back only as a subscription. When the song is free, you sell the door. Now it's happening to thinking. Chegg sold homework help. Since twenty twenty-one it has lost ninety-nine percent of its market value. Last year it let go nearly half its staff, and named the reason: the new realities of AI. Canva made design cheap enough for anyone. Now AI is making it nearly free, and this month Canva reported its first growth dip in ten years. The people who built those products didn't fail. The price left. And the value? In one experiment, the typical person wanted about seventeen thousand dollars a year to give up search engines. Something that costs them nothing. The price disappears. The value doesn't. If you want to follow where that leads, would you subscribe? Now, capitalism has an old answer to free: sell the door. Music did it. Search did it — free results, paid ads. So maybe capitalism doesn't end. Maybe it just moves. Watch where it moves to.

## Station 5 · What stays scarce (5:01–7:56)
It moves to the gates: the things that can't be copied. Land. Energy. The chips and the buildings that do the thinking. Raw materials. Your time. Trust. And you can already watch the money pool there. In the three months to July, Nvidia took in ninety-six billion dollars, nearly all of it from data centers. It kept about three quarters of every dollar as gross profit. That isn't villainy. That's what scarcity pays. Loudoun County, Virginia, is the largest cluster of data centers on Earth. An acre of land for one now goes for two to six million dollars. Even the people selling cheap intelligence say where this ends. Sam Altman, who runs OpenAI, wrote last year that "the cost of intelligence should eventually converge to near the cost of electricity." That's a prediction from someone with a stake. But notice what's left on the bill when thinking is free. The electricity. Something scarce. The pattern is older than AI. Across fourteen rich countries, about eighty percent of the rise in house prices since the Second World War came from one thing. The land underneath. Not the bricks. The dirt. Your rent doesn't fall because AI got cheap. Your rent is the scarce thing. Look at your own month: the AI that drafts your contract costs cents. The apartment you sign it in costs more every year. So is your attention: three companies collect more than half the advertising money outside China. Three companies hold sixty-three percent of the world's cloud market. Free search, free maps, free AI — and the door is owned. None of this is a collapse. Markets are doing exactly what they're built to do: follow the price. And when thinking is free, the price lives in the few things that stay scarce. So that's where money and power drift: toward whoever holds them. Not because anyone is evil. Because that's where the prices are. Personally, I think the danger was never that AI makes too much — it's that we leave what stays scarce to whoever gets there first. Someone saw this coming. In nineteen oh four, Lizzie Magie patented a board game to teach exactly that. Rent on land flows to whoever got there first, and everyone else goes broke. You know it as Monopoly. What got lost is that she wrote a second set of rules for the same board. Rules where everyone wins. Same board, two rulebooks. So, can we build a world where nearly everything is plentiful, and the few things that aren't are shared fairly?

## Station 6 · What comes after (7:56–9:45)
Yes. And the second rulebook is already being played, quietly, in a few places. It has two halves. First: what becomes plentiful becomes free for everyone. The United States built GPS and gave the signal to the world for nothing. By twenty seventeen, American companies alone had drawn more than a trillion dollars of value from it. Wikipedia is the same deal, for knowledge. Cheap intelligence is next: a free layer, like roads, that a kid in Lagos and a plumber in Ohio can build on alike. Second: what stays scarce, we run together. Land first, because nobody made it. Baden-Württemberg, in Germany, rewrote its property tax last year. You pay on the value of the land, not on what you build on it. Germany's highest tax court upheld it this April. Vienna came at it from the other side. Since twenty nineteen, when new land there is zoned for housing, usually two thirds of it has to be subsidized, affordable homes. And the city caps the land price. Whether it's enough is still argued. Energy: off the coast of Copenhagen, a wind farm has been shared since the year two thousand. Half belongs to the city's utility, half to about eight and a half thousand residents who bought in. And the data centers? Same question, no proven model yet at scale. That's the gap, and closing it is on governments, on the labs, and on the voters behind both. None of this takes anything from anyone. It's deciding, together, how to run the few things nobody made and all of us need.

## Station 7 · Ending (9:45–10:24)
Capitalism won't end with a bang. It ends quietly, price by price, every time one more thing you used to pay for is simply there, like air. The question was never whether. It's what we put in its place, and who's in the room when we decide. If you want to be in that room, subscribe — and tell me in the comments which thing you'd make free first. Lizzie Magie wrote two sets of rules. We've played the first for more than a century. The second is on the table now. Let's learn the rules.
