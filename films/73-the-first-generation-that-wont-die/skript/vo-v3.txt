Every generation before yours had to die.

Yours may be the first that won't.

Because labs now treat death as an engineering problem. And they've pointed A.I. at it.

So picture the day it works. The offer is in front of you. No more aging. No end date. Yes, or no.

If you could stop dying, would you?

Most people say no.

But the labs aren't waiting for an answer. Some want to copy a mind into a computer. And they've started. With a fly.

A real fly's brain was mapped, wire by wire. This year, a simple computer model built from that map began to steer a virtual body.

There's a second way to stop dying. Closer. Repair the body you already have. And it comes with a date. Twenty thirty-two.

The date comes from Ray Kurzweil, Google's longtime futurist.

His claim: a year back for every year you live.

That's what "won't die" means here. Not immortal. An accident could still kill you. Old age couldn't.

But Kurzweil has moved his date before, and he sells books about this future. Critics note that no peer-reviewed study stands behind it.

And at today's pace of progress, one blogger's analysis from this summer lands somewhere else entirely. A leading aging researcher shared it. Its result: not twenty thirty-two. Around twenty-four fifty-nine.

In your lifetime. Or four centuries too late.

And even that analysis says: no chance, unless A.I. speeds things up. So the first generation that won't die is a bet on A.I.

Is that bet paying off? There are early signs. A.I. models are learning to predict how a living cell reacts to a drug, before anyone runs the experiment. And in a study published this October, machine learning helped researchers pick out a single gene. They boosted it in old mice. The mice lived about fourteen percent longer.

A small study. Female mice only. Not yet repeated. And no drug against aging itself is approved anywhere.

So the date is a guess. The work is real.

But what if the repairs come too late for you?

Then the copy is what's left.

Back to that fly. This March, a San Francisco startup plugged that simple brain model into a virtual body. And the model began to choose. Walk. Groom. Feed.

The startup's headline: we've uploaded a fruit fly.

Critics said no. Even a foundation that works toward brain uploads. Their point: the model has none of the real fly's memories, and separate software does the walking.

The company's own chief executive is frank about how little went in.

And still. A map of a brain, switched on, choosing what a body does next.

The same executive says the first human uploads are a few years away, not decades. He also has a startup to fund. Yet of a human brain, science has mapped one cubic millimeter. A crumb. Its data filled more than a million gigabytes.

But suppose it worked. A copy wakes up with all your memories. Is that you? Or did you die, while something else remembers being you?

So ask it again. If the only way to stop dying were to become a copy, would you?

Which brings back the answer most people give. No.

In twenty thirteen, a major American survey asked about treatments to live to a hundred and twenty. Most said, "not for me." Yet most believed that other people would say yes.

It seems everybody wants this. Except everybody you ask.

What holds the yes back?

This August, a new survey turned the question around. Would you accept a shorter life, if it came without decline? No failing body. No fading mind. Almost two in three said yes.

So the fear isn't living forever. It's being old forever.

So ask it the fair way. If you could stop dying, and stay healthy, would you?

Even then, there's a serious case for no. One philosopher argued that an endless life ends in boredom. Others answer that some pleasures never wear out.

But suppose enough people say yes. What happens to a world that was built around dying?

Say it works. In this scenario, you're older than anyone alive today, and healthy.

Take a promise you may have made: till death do us part. For most of history, that usually meant less than thirty years. Now it has no end date. More time with the person you love. And a much bigger promise.

And a life that long has to be paid for. A London professor who studies long lives says the old plan, learn, work, retire, is breaking.

So you could have five careers. And you might never be done.

And what your parents would have left you? In this scenario, it never comes. Your parents stay. That's the gain. What they own stays with them. That's the price.

Still yes?

And an inheritance is only the small version. Power and ideas change hands the same way: when someone goes.

Max Planck, the physicist, saw it in science. A new truth, he wrote, doesn't win by convincing its opponents. It wins because they eventually die. Economists later tested that. When a star scientist died early, outsiders moved in, and did unusually strong work.

That doesn't make death good. It shows that people at the top can keep newcomers out, just by staying.

And at the very top, this subject has already come up. Beijing, September twenty twenty-five. A military parade, a live microphone. China's president and Russia's president walk toward the viewing platform.

Through his interpreter, Russia's president talks of organs transplanted again and again, of people growing younger, even immortal. China's president answers that some predict humans may live to a hundred and fifty this century.

This isn't about two countries. Any president. Any judge. Any founder. Who steps aside, if they never have to?

And who gets it at all? In that survey from twenty thirteen, a clear majority expected that only the wealthy would.

Now ask yourself. Would you still say yes, if the powerful said yes first? If you could afford it, and your neighbor couldn't?

It doesn't have to go that way. Start with what people actually said yes to. Not forever. Healthy.

Around the world, people spend close to ten years of their lives in poor health. Win those years back first, and you shrink the very thing people fear: the long decline.

But healthy years only count if people can afford them. Some medical technology gets cheap fast. Reading a human genome once cost about ninety-five million dollars. Today, a few hundred. Yet so far, in America, new medical technology has pushed health costs up, not down. Which pattern wins is still open.

Part of that comes down to rules. Labs don't write those. Voters and lawmakers do. A few exist already.

Who steps aside? American presidents must, after two terms. Who pays for longer lives? Denmark has tied its retirement age by law to how long people live.

And who can afford it? A Montana law now lets licensed clinics sell treatments after just a first safety trial. In return, a share of the profit must go to low-income patients. Supporters call that access. Critics call it a risk.

Most rules for a world like this have never been written. How many children, if so few of us go? One projection for Sweden suggests its population would grow more slowly than many fear. Even so, in this year's survey, one in five would accept stricter limits on having children, for a much longer life.

Would you?

So is yours the first generation that won't die?

The honest answer is a range. The estimates run from the next decade to four centuries from now. And neither the repair nor the copy has worked in a single human being yet.

But something has already changed. With A.I., death has moved from the things we accept to the things we work on. And what we work on, we have to think through, before it works. Do we want this? What does it do to a life? Who gets it?

If you could stop dying, would you?

Every generation before us was given the answer. We are the first to be asked the question.
