So if your brain can't catch up in time, what can?
Something can. Because the danger was never the machine.
It's the gap. The machine's clock is racing. The brain's clock barely moves.
But the brain isn't your only clock.
Two others can be set.
The slower takes a whole society. The faster takes a single person.
And you are carrying it right now.

The third reflex is the quiet one, and it makes us late.
Your brain expects tomorrow to look like today, plus a little.
But this machine doesn't add. It doubles. The length of a task AI can finish on its own doubles about every four months.
Even the experts missed. AI reached gold-medal level at the Math Olympiad five years before they expected it.
If even the experts were that far off on today's machines, whose gut is ready for superintelligence?
To be fair: what defines us, one researcher wrote this year, is how well we adapt.
True. But we adapt over years. The machine moves in months.
The scientist E. O. Wilson had a sentence for this, long before ChatGPT:
[CLIP K1: "…modern humanity is distinguished by Paleolithic emotions and medieval institutions like banks and religions and godlike technology."]
He wasn't talking about AI. But listen to the middle of his sentence. Institutions.
Rules. Laws. A seat belt for the brain: it holds, even when you aren't paying attention. So how fast do rules move?

Take Europe's AI law. It was proposed before ChatGPT existed. Its last rules won't apply until twenty twenty-eight.
More than seven years. For a machine on a four-month clock.
Not out of laziness: those rules were pushed back because the standards needed to apply them weren't ready.
So are rules just too slow for this?
One case says no. In the nineteen eighties, scientists reported a hole in the ozone layer. A little over two years later, a treaty was signed.
And that treaty has an update built in. It can be tightened without starting over. It has been, again and again.
The sky still needs decades to heal. But the rule came fast, and it kept learning.
So the slower clock can be set. It has been done. Just not in four months.

Is there anything that moves faster than a law?
There is. And it isn't out-thinking the machine. You won't, and you don't have to.
It's the clock you are carrying: how fast you notice what is happening inside you.
Yuval Noah Harari writes about AI, and has books to sell. He has a bigger word for that clock. And a price.
[CLIP C1: "…if for every $10 we spend on developing artificial intelligence we could invest at least $1 in developing our consciousness, I think we'll be okay. But this is not the current trajectory we are on."]
Developing our consciousness. What is that supposed to mean? A retreat on a mountain?
He means something plainer: knowing your own mind well enough to notice what a machine is doing to it.
Try something. Start a sentence in your head, and watch where the words come from.
You can't tell. Harari says he can't, either.
So much of your own mind runs unwatched. Developing consciousness means watching more of it. Not getting smarter. Noticing. This is fear. This is craving. This is something pulling at you.
Why now? Because you have competition, he says. Companies and governments are working to know you better than you know yourself. And whoever does can sell you anything. A product, or a politician.
The idea: a reflex you notice is harder to use against you.
So is meditation the answer? It's his. On his first course, he was told to do nothing but notice his breath. He couldn't manage more than ten seconds.
Whether that is your way stays open. The smallest version takes one breath, between the headline and your thumb. It hands the decision back to you, before a headline tuned for clicks takes it.
But one person noticing is the small version. Harari's dollar is meant for the big one: understanding the mind itself. And look where the money goes.
This year, four companies are spending more than seven hundred billion dollars, mostly on AI. America's flagship brain research program gets about four hundred million.
Those pots aren't the same thing, and his ratio is a wish, not a calculation. But they show where the attention is.
And what we don't understand, we don't measure. AI gets graded on how smart it is. Harari asks a different question: what does it do to the person using it?
A first test exists. Researchers put everyday questions to a range of chatbots. Nearly all failed to respect the user's attention. Told to ignore the user's wellbeing, about two thirds turned harmful.
So that is what the dollar is for. Noticing yourself. Studying the mind. And grading the machine on what it does to us.
But one calm person doesn't slow a race. What if you get faster, and the rules don't?

Then you notice more, and the race runs on. So set both clocks, and picture an evening a few years from now.
The headline still arrives. Your pulse still jumps. But in this version, the app in your hand carries a grade. Not for how clever it is. For what it does to the people who use it.
It was tested before release, and again every few months. When something goes wrong, the rule changes within the year. And you know your reflexes by name.
Far away? Take that evening apart.
The grade? You just heard a first test.
Testing before release? Demis Hassabis, a co-founder of Google DeepMind, has proposed a body that tests the most powerful models before launch. The tests would be renewed, perhaps every quarter.
Rules that move within a year? Last month, the head of the United Nations called for a global framework for AI risks, within one year.
And the pressure is there. In twenty seventeen, three world leaders raised concerns about AI in their speeches at the United Nations. This year, a hundred and thirty-five did.
None of it is settled. The framework is not a treaty. The testing body would be paid mostly by the industry it tests. And it is not the course we're on. Harari says so himself.
But the race is a product of choices. So is the gap.
Labs can test before they ship. Governments can write rules with the update built in. Voters can demand both. And one clock needs no vote at all.
