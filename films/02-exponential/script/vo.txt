In nineteen seventy-five, two Dutch researchers asked people to guess where a doubling process ends up. Often two thirds of them guessed less than a tenth of the real answer.
Even when they were told how doubling works, it barely helped.
And it doesn't only fool ordinary people.

Every year, the International Energy Agency publishes a big outlook on the world's energy.
For more than a decade, it kept showing new solar power each year growing for a bit, and then levelling off.

They drew a flat line. Reality bent upward. So the next year they drew another flat line.

Solar runs on a loop.
Every time the world doubles the number of solar panels it has ever made, they get about twenty percent cheaper.
Cheaper panels mean more buyers. More buyers mean more panels.
The result feeds its own cause. That's what makes growth compound.
Since the seventies, the price of a solar panel has fallen by more than ninety-nine percent.

AI is running on loops like that. Several of them, stacked.
The computing power used to train the leading AI models has grown four to five times a year since twenty ten.
Running them gets cheaper even faster. On one test of PhD-level science questions, the same quality of answer has been getting about forty times cheaper per year.
And the tasks AI can finish on its own, at least half the time, keep getting longer. Measured in how long they'd take a person, that length has doubled roughly every seven months. Lately it may be speeding up.

So where's the turning point? Where does the curve bend?

So how do we get there? Start with the cheapest fix there is. Learn to read the curve.
In March twenty twenty, as the pandemic began, psychologists found that most people pictured the virus spreading in a straight line.
Then they walked some of them through how doubling works, step by step. Their guesses got better. And they backed the measures to slow the spread more strongly.
Back in the seventies, a quick instruction barely helped. Working through the doublings yourself seems to.
And knowing still isn't doing. But it costs almost nothing.
So next time you hear a number about AI, ask one question. How long does it take to double?

But reading the curve doesn't stop anything. For that you need brakes, and they should be tied to the curve, not the calendar.
In twenty twenty-four, sixteen AI companies met in Seoul and signed voluntary commitments. Each would publish its own danger lines in advance. And in the extreme, if it couldn't keep a model's risks below those lines, it wouldn't build or release that model at all.
In May twenty twenty-five, Anthropic was about to release a new model. For the first time, its tests couldn't rule out that the model might give real help to someone trying to build a chemical or biological weapon.
It hadn't proven the line was crossed. It just couldn't rule it out anymore. So it launched with stricter safeguards anyway, as a precaution.
A tripwire like that doesn't care how fast the curve is moving. It goes off when the measurement says so.
The catch is that the companies mostly grade themselves, and they can rewrite their own rules. Anthropic did, early this year. It dropped its promise to pause if its safeguards weren't ready, arguing that one lab can't hold the line alone. Critics called it a broken promise.
Which is why we think tripwires belong in law, tied to measurements instead of dates.

Brakes only tell the curve where not to go. Where should it go?
For fifty years, working out how a protein folds was one of biology's great unsolved problems.
Then an AI called AlphaFold predicted the structures of virtually all of the two hundred million proteins researchers know about. Two of its makers shared a Nobel Prize for it.
More than two million people in a hundred and ninety countries have used that map.
A map isn't a medicine. Most of the slow work is still ahead.
But it's a good question for any field, including yours. Which problem is still waiting for its map?

And then there's the question of who gets to ride the curve.
Remember the solar loop? In twenty twenty-four, Pakistan imported about seventeen gigawatts of solar panels. Twice as much as the year before.
Mostly for rooftops. Shops, offices, families escaping high power bills.
Nobody planned it from above. Panels got cheap enough, and people did the math.
It's messy. The national grid now sells less power, and the government has been rethinking the deal it gives rooftop owners. And a panel still needs a roof, and the money to buy it.
AI is getting cheaper even faster than solar did. Who gets it first, and on what terms, hasn't been decided yet.
