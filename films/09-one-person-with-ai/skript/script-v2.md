Working title: One Person With AI Will Beat a Billion-Dollar Company (a prediction, never stated as fact)
Insight: Artificial intelligence doesn't level the field, it amplifies it. Whether the one-person company lifts one person or the many is decided by a few choices we make now, not by the technology.

Basis: geruest-v3.md (approved), faktenzettel.md (only these facts), hook-bauplan.md (binding first 60 s), vibe-brief.md, feedback-log.md.

## Hook variants (all follow the hook plan: promise → proof clip → open question → roadmap → first win)

**Variant A · Let's build it (FAVOURITE, used below)**
Let's build a billion-dollar company.
Just you, a kitchen table, and artificial intelligence.
No office. No staff. No investors.
→ The title promise in plain words, "you" from the second line, the kitchen table as the thumbnail motif. Pays off in the ending ("on ours"). To cover "beat" in the title: the glass tower stands visibly opposite the table in the first frame.

**Variant B · From joke to forecast**
One person. One laptop. One billion dollars.
That used to be a joke.
Now the people building artificial intelligence call it a forecast.
→ Rhythm hook. "You" arrives late; the guide format has to be set up separately.

**Variant C · The question to you**
What if the next billion-dollar company has exactly one employee?
And what if that employee is you?
No office. No investors. Just you and artificial intelligence.
→ Direct "you". Risk: sounds like a get-rich ad.

All three continue identically from the clip onward.

## 1 · Step one: your team (1:05–1:45)
VO:
Step one.
You, at the kitchen table, with an idea.
And around you, quietly, a team appears.
One writes the code. One designs.
One answers your customers at three in the morning.

Companies got big because working together was expensive. [S3]
Now it's getting cheap.
And that means you can go small.
You can build for a problem only forty people have.
For a corporation, that's nothing.
For you, it's a start.

But if you can hire this team in an afternoon, so can everyone else.
Keep that in mind.

PICTURE: Your hands on the table; sketches, words and a first screen fill the paper as if many quiet hands were helping (warm light, never robots) → the glass tower across the street: its walls dissolve into a web of threads that thin out (name card: Ronald Coase, 1937) → zoom into one hospital ward, a nurse frowning at a paper roster → pull back: thousands of kitchen tables light up at once.

## 4 · The fork: millions follow the same guide (second reveal) (2:47–4:13)
VO:
Now imagine you're not the only one following this guide.
Millions are.
Remember: everyone can hire the same team.
So picture two worlds.

In the first, a few superstars win almost everything.
Everyone else rents the same tools from a handful of companies, and the gap keeps growing.
Your table still glows.
It's just one light in a flood that nobody sees.
And we're already drifting this way.
In America, the smallest businesses use these tools less than big ones. [S12]
So do workers in the Global South. [S13]
Both gaps are growing. [S12] [S13]

Now the second world.
The floor rises.
A nurse builds the rota tool her ward has needed for years.
A farmer in Kenya gets advice in his own language, on a simple phone.
A teacher in India builds an exercise for the student who keeps falling behind.
Millions of small workshops, each run by someone who knows a problem by heart.
And the giants don't disappear.
They become the roads everyone drives on.

Pause here.
Which world would you bet on?

So here's the answer.
The technology doesn't decide this.
The same tools lead to both worlds.
A few choices do.
And we're making them right now.

ON SCREEN (World A): US firms under 5 employees vs large firms (Census 2026) · Global North 1 in 4, Global South 1 in 7 (Microsoft 2025).
PICTURE: Your kitchen table multiplies into millions of tables across a dark globe → World A (calm, quiet): a few blazing towers, small lights fading around them, one thick cable bundle feeding every table → World B (warm, sensory): a nurse at night showing colleagues her simple rota app, they laugh; a farmer in a field reading a text on a basic phone; a teacher and a boy bent over one exercise, he gets it → towers become lit roads between millions of small warm lights → silence, both worlds side by side → they merge into one fork in the road.
LOOP: Harvests "millions" (hook) and "so can everyone else" (step 1). Viewer chooses (pause). Second reveal: choices, not technology. Opens: which choices?

## 5 · How the many win (longest part) (4:13–8:02)
VO:
Which choices?
Four answers: one for each person in that second world, and one for you.
Each one is already being tried.
None of them is finished.

One.
Someone at your side.
Think of the farmer with his simple phone.
More advice alone won't carry him far.
The struggling owners in Kenya had advice.
What they may have needed was someone to help them choose.
Something like that did help. [S14]
Women running small businesses in Kenya were paired with experienced local businesswomen as mentors. [S14]
Their profits rose by about a fifth. [S14]
The effect faded over time, and mentors don't scale like software. [S14]
But the same pattern shows up in a classroom in Nigeria. [S15]
Students learned English with an artificial intelligence tutor, and their teachers right beside them. [S15]
In six weeks they made big gains, and girls gained the most. [S15]
So give the farmer the machine and a person.
A mentor, a neighbour, a co-op, someone a few steps ahead.

Two.
Access for everyone.
The teacher in India, building that exercise?
She needs computing power she can afford.
India now offers it to startups and small firms for less than a dollar an hour. [S16]
Britain offers every adult a free course in artificial intelligence, aiming for ten million workers by twenty thirty. [S17]
Does it work? Nobody knows yet. It's a bet. [S16] [S17]
But it's the right kind of bet.
Treat computing power like a road.
The public builds it.
Everyone drives on it, including her.

Three.
Tools nobody can switch off.
Remember: your team is rented.
One startup, Windsurf, lost direct access to the models it was building on. [S9]
With less than five days' notice. [S9]
The landlord was the company from that first clip. [S9]
Five days is fine for a hobby.
Not for a livelihood.
So the nurse's rota tool should run on a model nobody can take away, with the freedom to switch.
The World Bank makes a similar case: small, cheap tools, and no dependence on a single provider. [S18]
Open tools still cost money to run, and fair rules take years to write.
But a tool that runs on the cheapest phone works for almost everyone.
Including the nurse on the night shift.

Four.
Safety to take a risk.
And you?
What would it take for you to jump?
Most people only jump when there's a floor beneath them.
Not because they're timid. Because they have rent to pay.
When France let unemployed people keep their benefits while starting a company, many more people started one. [S19]
And those companies weren't worse. [S19]
It's a safety net you keep while you try.
That's one country and one policy, and it costs public money.
But it shows what a floor does.
It lets people try.

None of this happens by itself.
The artificial intelligence companies decide prices, access, and how much notice you get.
Governments decide who gets computing power, mentors, and a floor to land on.
Schools decide whether your kids learn to build, or only to use.
Investors decide whether they back one superstar or ten thousand small workshops.
And you decide too: who you teach, who you hire, whose tool you try.
Right now, most of this is pilots and promises.
That's the distance between where we are and the second world.

ON SCREEN: (1) Brooks et al., AEJ Applied 2018 · World Bank, Benin City. (2) IndiaAI: under $1 per GPU-hour · UK: free AI skills courses, 10 million workers by 2030. (3) Quote card, World Bank WDR 2026: "small AI". (4) Hombert et al., Journal of Finance 2020.
PICTURE: Four doors open one after another, each opening on a World B person. (1) The farmer in his field; an older farmer walks over and they look at one phone together → a classroom in Benin City, Nigeria, a teacher leaning over a girl's screen, she grins. (2) The teacher in India at dusk, her laptop lights up → a road being built, and many people walking onto it, her among them; a British evening class of adults of every age. (3) Your table's single cable to the tower: the plug is pulled, the table goes dark for a beat → the cable splits into many cables to many sources, the light returns → the nurse on a night shift, her rota tool on a cheap phone. (4) The camera turns to you: you stand at the edge of a jump, a soft net appears below, you step. → Hands on one table: a lab badge, a parliament pass, a school bell, an investor's pen, your own hand.
LOOP: Closes "which choices?" with four answers, each with what, evidence, limit (mid-block) and back to a person. Names responsibility (labs, governments, schools, investors, you). Opens the last loop: what was missing from the guide?
