A.I.-native companies are rising. They have almost no employees. They never forget anything. And they keep working while everyone sleeps. Some say soon one of them will be worth a billion dollars, run by a single person. How is that even possible? To understand it, we have to take one apart.

Organ by organ. Its memory. Its hands. Its will. And then the one question that decides who gets to have one.

First, the memory. In an ordinary company, knowledge lives in people's heads. When someone leaves, a piece of the company leaves with them. An A.I.-native company keeps everything. Every meeting, every message, every decision, every mistake, in one place. Silicon Valley calls it the company brain. Tom Blomfield, then a partner at Y Combinator, which funds young startups, told founders to transcribe literally every meeting. Then, he says, your entire company becomes queryable. He has since moved to Anthropic, a lab that builds artificial intelligence. [pause] But a memory alone does nothing. Who acts on it?

The hands. They're agents: artificial intelligence that doesn't just answer, but acts. They read the memory, do the work, and write what they learned back into it. And they don't stop at night. At Y Combinator, one agent answers the team's data questions all day. At night, a second one goes looking for everything that failed.

[CLIP: Blomfield, approx. 8:45–8:57, "that second AI agent overnight goes and puts in pull requests to fix all of the problems from the day before. And so, if you go back as a human on the second day and run the same query as yesterday, it now works."]

Nobody told it what to fix. It found the problems itself. Every night, the tool proposes its own repairs. Every morning, it works a little better than the day before. Something that learns in its sleep. This is the part I can't stop thinking about. Jack Dorsey, who runs the payments company Block, and Roelof Botha, who sits on Block's board, wrote it like this, a few weeks after Block cut about four thousand jobs.

[QUOTE CARD: Jack Dorsey & Roelof Botha, March 2026, "the intelligence lives in the system"]

The intelligence lives in the system. Not in a chain of managers, passing messages up and down. But if the intelligence lives in the system, and the hands never sleep, what are people still for?

For the one thing the system can't give itself. A will. The memory knows what happened. The hands can act. But neither knows what's worth doing. Somebody has to decide what should exist. Which problem matters. When something is good, and when it's wrong. Somebody has to want something, and care how it turns out. That's the third organ, and it's human. Think of a conductor in front of an orchestra. The conductor plays no instrument. Yet without one, there's no music. Only noise. In an A.I.-native company, that's where the people stand. Not inside the machine. In front of it. Which raises the obvious question. How many conductors does one orchestra need?

Maybe only one. Put the three organs together. A memory that forgets nothing. Hands that never sleep. And one will in front. [pause] In principle, that's a whole company. No departments. No layers. A memory, hands, and someone who wants something. Big companies are already leaning this way. At Shopify, a team that wants another person first has to show why artificial intelligence can't do the job. But the purest form is tiny. In Austin, a man who had been laid off started a company with two partners and about three hundred dollars. This spring, the company said it ran on the three of them and twelve agents. And Dario Amodei, who runs the lab Anthropic, was asked when the first billion-dollar company with one person would appear.

[QUOTE CARD: Dario Amodei, May 2025, on the first one-person billion-dollar company: "2026"]

Twenty twenty-six. It's a prediction, and as far as anyone can show, nobody has done it yet. But now you can see how it could be possible. So does it actually work?

Not by itself. In the first half of this year, about five hundred and sixty thousand new apps landed in Apple's App Store. About twice as many as in the same months the year before. Downloads? Up by two percent. Building got cheap. Getting noticed didn't. And in Kenya, researchers gave six hundred and forty small business owners an artificial intelligence advisor on WhatsApp. On average, nothing changed. Those already doing well did about fifteen percent better. Those who were struggling did almost ten percent worse. Similar advice. Different choices. The hands amplify whatever will stands in front of them. [pause]

So here's the real question. If anyone can lead a company like this, will many people become conductors? Or will a few become billionaires, while everyone else watches from outside? If you could decide, which world would you build? [pause] I don't know which one we'll get. Nobody does. But the technology doesn't choose. We do.

So how do many people learn to conduct? Not alone. Remember Kenya? An older study there, from before these tools, tried something simpler. Women running small businesses got a mentor: an experienced local businesswoman. Their profits rose by about twenty percent. The effect faded later. But it points somewhere. Maybe what a struggling conductor needs isn't a smarter machine. It's a person who has walked the road. Any company, any town, any guild of craftspeople could organize that. Now imagine that at scale. Companies that spend the savings on time to learn, not only on cuts. First one agent. Then five. Then a whole orchestra. It's Shopify's rule, turned around. Not what can artificial intelligence do instead of people. But what can people do with it. Does that work at scale? Nobody has proven it yet. Boards decide where the savings go. But even a trained conductor needs instruments. Who gets them?

The World Bank has one answer. In its new World Development Report, it argues for small, cheap tools that work over a text message or a simple phone. Picture a whole orchestra that fits into a basic handset. In a market town. On a farm. In a workshop with one person in it. India went further. Its public program offers computing power to startups and small firms for about a dollar per chip, per hour. Does that create new conductors? Nobody has measured it yet. But any country, any city, any school could try the same. The World Bank adds one warning, though. Don't let everyone depend on a single provider. And remember Kenya: cheap tools alone didn't lift the ones who were struggling. That's why the mentor comes first, and the tools second. Because the hands are only half of it. The memory matters just as much. Whose is it?

Today, a company brain belongs to whoever owns the company. Does it have to? Imagine the bakeries of one city sharing a memory they own together. Every small bakery, remembering as much as a supermarket chain. We couldn't find anyone who has built a shared company brain like that yet. But the pieces exist. There's a fully open model called Apertus. Anyone can see how it's built, and use it. And there's a cooperative called Read Co-op, owned by its members, that runs its own artificial intelligence for reading old handwriting. So a brain doesn't have to belong to one owner. It can belong to everyone who feeds it. And then the people who feed it also decide what goes in, and what stays out. Who makes that happen? Lawmakers, who write the rules. Governments, who fund the tools. And you, every time you choose which company to build, join, or buy from.

So now we can put it back together. [pause] An A.I.-native company is a living thing with three organs. A memory that forgets nothing. Hands that work through the night. And a will in front, deciding what's worth making. In its purest form, that will is one person. For two thousand years, Roelof Botha says, humans have organized themselves in roughly the same way. That's what is changing now. Maybe someone will build a billion-dollar company alone. But that's not the question that matters. The question is whether a few people get these organs, or millions do. Honestly? I'd rather see a million small orchestras than one giant one. [pause] So if the memory and the hands were yours tomorrow, what would you conduct? Tell me in the comments.
