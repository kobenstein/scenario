# Film 4 · Retention critique of Text v1 · "You Don't Have to Work Anymore"

Critic's pass, 2026-10-19. I watched it in my head on a phone, half distracted. Timings assume ~145 wpm plus ~1.5 s per `[pause]`, which gives about 9:40 of VO.

## Verdict

1. The bones are right. The cold open is a real scene, the "You don't have to…" refrain works, the Sunday-evening plant pays off, and Jakob's core line lands at 6:02. This is not a report.
2. It leaks in four places: the fact flip comes at 0:51 (too late), the hook's promise ("it doesn't go where you think") is broken by a slump that goes exactly where you think, the turn has a logic hole a skeptic will spot, and 4:04–6:54 is a list of nice things connected by "and then".
3. The 80-years peak is good but buried. A Maslow lecture and four more questions follow it, then a recap. Fix the top 10 below and this is a strong film. No full rewrite is needed.

## Top 10 fixes, ranked by retention impact

### 1 · Move the fact flip in the hook to ≤ 30 s and cut the warm-up (0:24–0:51)
**Quoted (Hook A / §0):**
> But that's not the interesting part.
> The interesting part is what happens to you after that.
> Month three.
> Year two.
> Because something shifts inside a person who no longer has to.
> And it doesn't go where you think.
> [pause]
> It sounds like a daydream.
> But some of the most powerful people building artificial intelligence are saying, out loud, that this morning is coming.

**Problem:** "That's not the interesting part / the interesting part is" is an 8-second announcement. A skeptical viewer's first thought, at around 0:10, is "fantasy, click". The answer ("this is real") comes at 0:51, and it is an anonymous wave of the hand ("some of the most powerful people"), not a face. Kurzgesagt flips fiction to fact around 0:15–0:20. Also, "a person who no longer has to" leaves the ear waiting for an object that never comes.

**Rewrite (keeps A's scene and borrows B's best move, the real voice):**
> Tomorrow morning, you wake up.
> No alarm.
> On your phone, one message.
> Your income is taken care of.
> Not for a month. Not for a year.
> For the rest of your life.
> [pause]
> You don't have to work anymore.
> Ever.
> [pause]
> So what do you do?
> Sleep in. Travel. Finally rest.
> And around month three, most people hit a wall.
> The reason isn't the one you'd guess.
> [pause]
> It sounds like a daydream.
> It's what the people building AI are now saying on stage.
> CLIP: Elon Musk, "My prediction is that work will be optional."

This puts the title promise at about 0:16 (as now), the open loop at about 0:24 (now 0:40) and the flip with a real face at about 0:32 (now 0:51). "Taken care of" is warmer than "secured", which sounds like bank language.

### 2 · Pay off the hook's promise: the slump goes exactly where the viewer expected (2:26–2:52)
**Quoted:**
> And it doesn't go where you think. *(hook)*
> This is the fear almost everyone has about a world without work. That we'd just numb ourselves. *(§3)*

**Problem:** The hook says "not where you think". At 2:30 the film shows the couch, the wine and the scrolling, which is precisely what everyone thinks, and then even says so. The viewer feels the bait-and-switch ("I knew it") and the loop deflates before the turn arrives. The surprise is the reason, not the slump. Make the hook promise that (fix 1 does), then pay it explicitly at the turn.

**Rewrite at the turn:**
> Maybe not because we're lazy.
> Maybe because we're exhausted.
> That's the part almost nobody expects.

### 3 · Close the logic hole in the turn and say Jakob's thesis out loud (2:53–4:01)
**Quoted:**
> Why do we numb ourselves now? … Maybe because we're exhausted. Because for most of our lives, we've lived in a world of have-to. And when the have-to grinds you down, you need somewhere to escape.
> …
> Researchers followed unemployed people into retirement. Their days didn't change at all. But they became noticeably happier. Same days. Different label.
> So the slump isn't the end of the story.

**Problem:** A skeptic asks, "But in your scene the have-to is already gone, so why is the freed person still on the couch with a glass at 4 p.m.?" The script never answers. It gives two mechanisms (the cage in Rat Park, the label in the retirement study) and ties neither back to the slump. It also never states the payoff: take away the have-to and you take away the reason to escape. "Followed unemployed people into retirement" is hard to parse by ear. "Isn't the end of the story" is a worn phrase.

**Rewrite (same length, both mechanisms land):**
> And when the have-to grinds you down for twenty years, you don't stop being tired the morning it ends.
> The habit of escaping outlasts the thing you were escaping from.
> That's the slump.
> But it's a hangover, not a destiny.
> One famous experiment suggested something similar.
> Rats alone in an empty cage kept drinking the morphine water.
> Rats in a rich park, full of space and friends, mostly left it alone.
> Maybe the problem was never the rat.
> Maybe it was the cage.
> And sometimes the cage is only a word.
> In Germany, researchers watched people who were long-term unemployed turn sixty-five.
> Nothing about their days changed.
> But overnight, they got happier.
> They weren't "unemployed" anymore. They were "retired".
> Now imagine a world where nobody has to work.
> Nobody is unemployed.
> The word is gone.
> [pause]
> Take away the have-to, and you take away the reason to run.
> What's left is a question.
> Who am I, if I'm not my job?

(Study: Hetschko, Knabe & Schöb 2014, German SOEP data. Check the retirement-age detail and the wording before locking.)

### 4 · §1 is a clip collage, then 30 s of warm-up. Plant the ownership question here (0:54–1:44)
**Quoted:**
> CLIP Musk / CLIP Hassabis / CLIP Suleyman
> Listen to the words they lean on. If it goes well.
> But for the next few minutes, imagine it does. Imagine a superintelligence does the work…
> Then here's the idea this whole film is about.
> And the difference between them might decide what kind of life you get to live.

**Problem:** Three CEO voices in a row is the clip-collage pattern the top-performer analysis says to avoid. The Suleyman clip also repeats the hook's question word for word. "They lean on" is plural, but only Hassabis says "if". "Imagine… imagine… here's the idea this whole film is about" is three warm-ups in 15 s. "Might decide what kind of life you get to live" is vague. The biggest issue: "the wealth it creates reaches everyone" is the film's hugest assumption and goes by in one line, while "Who owns it?" appears out of nowhere at 9:08. That is a loop opened and left in the same 40 seconds.

**Rewrite (Musk has moved into the hook):**
> CLIP: Demis Hassabis, "If everything goes well, then we should be in an era of what I like to call radical abundance."
> If everything goes well.
> Nobody can promise that.
> The machines could do the work. The hard part is whether the wealth reaches you.
> That's the biggest "if" in this film. Keep it in mind.
> But for the next few minutes, let's say it does.
> [pause]
> AI won't end work.
> It will end having to work.
> Those sound alike.
> They're not.
> One of them scares almost everyone.
> The other could change what a human life is for.

The last line plants §7. Suleyman's quote can go on a quote card at the very end, or be dropped.

### 5 · The mid-film sag (4:04–6:54): replace "and then" links with but/therefore friction
**Quoted links:**
> And that question is where everything begins. *(4:04)*
> Then, a different question at parties. *(5:07)*
> And now imagine what wanting to looks like… *(6:11)*
> Which leads all the way up. *(6:54)*

**Problem:** That is 2:50 of "here's another nice thing". Each section is a list, and the joints are "and / then / and now / which leads". Nothing pushes back, so the phone viewer's thumb starts moving around 4:40. Every joint needs a "but" or a "therefore" that raises a small new question.

**Rewrites for the joints:**
- §3→4: *"That question sounds frightening. It's actually the door."* (then straight into the free-time study)
- §4→5: *"But people aren't enough. Sooner or later, someone at the table asks you the old question. What do you do? And for once, you get to answer it differently."*
- §5→6: *"But wanting to has a catch. Now nothing stops you. Not money. Not permission. Only you."*
- §6→7: *"But give long enough, and you run into a question no project can answer."*

### 6 · §6 turns into tech hype and machine imagery (6:04–6:54, about 50 s on one idea)
**Quoted:**
> …with the most powerful help any human has ever had.
> One person. A thousand AI agents. One idea.
> You can point the most capable mind on Earth at it yourself.
> Millions of small labs, in kitchens and garages.
> The unemployment office becomes a purpose office.
> A whole generation of explorers.

**Problem:** This is the only stretch where the film starts sounding like an AI keynote: superlatives ("most powerful", "most capable mind on Earth"), agents as the subject, a slogan stack. Jakob's rule is no machine imagery and the person at the center. A doom-or-hype-allergic viewer leaves here. The daughter is the only beat that carries any feeling, and it gets three hype lines on top of it. "Purpose office" is also illogical, because in this world there is no unemployment office.

**Rewrite:**
> One person, one idea, and help no scientist had ten years ago.
> Until now, a big idea needed money, a company, permission.
> Now it needs you, and a reason.
> Imagine your daughter has a rare illness no company would ever research.
> You don't have to wait anymore.
> You have the time. You have the help.
> And you have the best reason there is.
> [pause]
> Now multiply that by every kitchen table on Earth.
> And slowly, what earns respect starts to change.
> Not what you earn.
> What you give.

### 7 · Protect the peak: one number, a clear first person, end §7 on the summit (7:33–8:46)
**Quoted:**
> A human life, if you're lucky, lasts about four thousand weeks. … You're only here for about eighty years.
> So what's the most beautiful thing you could do with them?
> What would let you say, at the very end: I had a wonderful life. And I brought good into this world.
> Near the end of his life, Maslow placed something above becoming yourself. Going beyond yourself. Maybe that's where this road leads. Not to what you do. To who you're becoming. What is this life for? Why are you here at all? No job can answer that for you. No machine. Not this film. For most of history, there was no time to even ask. Soon, there might be.

**Problems:**
- The same number appears twice in two units, 25 s apart ("four thousand weeks", "eighty years"). That's a recap inside the climax.
- "Most beautiful thing" is a literal translation of "das Schönste" and sounds soft in English.
- On a cheap TTS the colon is inaudible, so the narrator suddenly *claims* "I had a wonderful life." That confuses a listener at the exact moment it should hit hardest.
- Right after the peak comes a past-looking academic aside (Maslow's late theory), then the "spiritual guru" vocabulary Jakob warned about ("going beyond yourself", "who you're becoming"), then four questions and a recap of 7:02 ("for most of history…"). The peak is spent 30 s before the section ends.

**Rewrite:**
> So let time stop for a moment.
> A human life, if you're lucky, is about four thousand weeks.
> Eighty years.
> Some of yours are already behind you.
> *(Bronnie Ware beat as is.)*
> [pause]
> So what would you do with the ones you have left?
> What would you need to do, so that one day, at the very end, you could look back and say it?
> [pause] [softly]
> "I had a wonderful life.
> And I brought good into this world."
> [long pause, about 3 s, music only]
> What is this life for?
> No job can answer that for you.
> Not this film either.
> For most of history, there was no time to ask.
> Soon, there might be.

Cut the Maslow line. If Jakob wants it, move it before Bronnie Ware as one line ("Maslow, late in life, put one thing above all the others: living for something bigger than yourself.") and never after the peak. Put "I had a wonderful life…" on screen as text too, so the first person survives a flat voice.

### 8 · Ending: cut the recap and the meta line, end on one question (8:48–9:39)
**Quoted:**
> This future is possible.
> Some of the most powerful people on Earth say it's where we're heading.
> But it won't arrive by itself.
> … That isn't a question for engineers. It's a question for all of us.
> And then the sentence will finally be complete.
> So how do we get from here to there?
> If you never had to work again, what would you do with your four thousand weeks?

**Problem:** The first two lines repeat 0:51 and 1:15, so the first 8 seconds of the ending are a recap, which is where end-screen viewers drop. "Won't arrive by itself" and "a question for all of us" are stock phrases. "The sentence will finally be complete" is an "and then" and also a meta comment. Nobody remembers that it was a "sentence". The two final questions cancel each other: the comment question sounds like comment bait and steals the cliffhanger that should send people to the next film. The good news is there's no announcement and no subscribe plea, which is correct.

**Rewrite:**
> Remember the biggest "if"?
> The same superintelligence could free everyone.
> Or make a few people richer than anyone has ever been.
> The difference isn't in the code.
> It's one question.
> Who owns it?
> That gets decided by laws, by votes, by what people demand. Not in a lab.
> [pause]
> Because one morning, maybe soon, you could wake up to that message.
> You don't have to.
> [pause]
> You want to.
> [pause]
> So how do we get from here to there?
> *(hold, end screen)*

Move "If you never had to work again, what would you do with your four thousand weeks?" to the pinned comment and/or the description.

### 9 · §4 opens with 11 s of nothing, then puts two numbers in the ear (4:04–4:29)
**Quoted:**
> And that question is where everything begins.
> Because once the ground beneath you is safe, something opens up.
> Not one thing.
> Everything, all at once.
> Here's a curious thing about free time.
> It makes people happier, up to about two hours a day.
> Past five, it can drop.

**Problem:** Three abstract warm-up lines, then "Here's a curious thing" (another warm-up), then two numbers. "Past five" leaves the ear asking "five what?". To a skeptic, "free time makes you less happy" also sounds like evidence *against* the film unless the condition comes first.

**Rewrite:**
> That question sounds frightening. It's actually the door.
> Researchers asked thousands of people how much free time makes them happiest.
> More helps, up to a point.
> After that, it can start to hurt.
> But only when the time is spent alone, on nothing that matters.
> Which tells you exactly where to go.
> First, to people.

(This avoids two numbers. Put "2 h / 5 h" in the picture as a curve. Source: Sharif, Mogilner & Hershfield 2021.)

### 10 · Sweep out the tics and stock phrases (whole script)
These are small individually, but together they make the narrator sound templated, and a cheap TTS makes the repetition obvious.
- **"Here's…" ×5:** "here's the idea this whole film is about", "Here's a curious thing", "here's the twist", "Here's the difference", "Here's something we rarely say out loud". Keep at most one ("Here's the difference.").
- **"Not X. Y." ×15+:** keep the ones that carry meaning ("Not what you earn. What you give.", "Not work. Having to."). Cut "Not one thing. Everything, all at once.", "Not to what you do. To who you're becoming.", "Not on the couch."
- **"out loud" ×2** (hook, §7 opener). Cut the §7 opener "Here's something we rarely say out loud." Start on "For almost all of human history…".
- **Stock phrases:** "isn't the end of the story", "find your people", "it won't arrive by itself", "a question for all of us", "What makes you come alive?" (self-help poster, try "What would you do even if nobody paid you?"), "Waiting for Friday" (Hook C).
- **Hype:** "Poverty starts to sound like a word from a history book" (say "Nobody on your street is one bad month from losing their home." It's concrete and not a prophecy), "A band on every street" (cheesy, and the picture already shows it), "Everything, all at once".
- **Kenya "Because" fallacy:** "Many started their own businesses. Because most of the moments when people feel fully absorbed… happen at work." This implies Kenyans started businesses *because of* flow research. Change "Because" to "And there's a reason that's not surprising:". Also soften "most of the moments" to "more of those moments happen at work than on the couch" (Csikszentmihalyi & LeFevre 1989 compared work and leisure, not "most").

### 4 · Emotional arc

- **Does the 80-years peak land?** Almost. The setup is excellent: the zoom-out to "survival ate their days", then "Some of yours are already behind you" (the line that makes it personal), then Ware's "I wish I hadn't worked so hard", a dry irony that fits this exact film. It then fails in delivery: the number repeats, the first person collapses on a flat voice, and 30 s of lecture follow the summit. With fix #7 it should be the moment people screenshot.
- **Missing thread:** the peak's second half, "I brought good into this world", is Jakob's "giving" idea. §6 sets it up ("Not what you earn. What you give."). That's a good plant. With fix #6's joint ("give long enough, and you run into a question…") the connection becomes felt rather than coincidental.
- **Does the slump→turn convince a skeptic?** Not yet. A skeptic hears "people would just numb themselves". The script agrees, then asks "why do we numb ourselves now?", which is a great question and Jakob's best idea in the film. But the answer ("exhausted", "the cage", "the label") explains *today's* numbing, not the numbing the film just showed *after* freedom. The skeptic's comment writes itself: "So why were they drinking at 4 p.m. in your own example?" Fix #3 closes it: the slump is a hangover from the have-to, not the destination, and the "unemployed" stigma, which is half of why jobless people suffer, cannot exist when nobody has to work. Say the thesis in one sentence: "Take away the have-to, and you take away the reason to run."
- **Optional, strong evidence for skeptics (verify before use):** lottery winners are real people who "got the message". Swedish studies (Cesarini et al. 2017; Lindqvist, Östling & Cesarini 2020) found that most big winners kept working, cutting their hours only modestly, and reported higher life satisfaction even years later. One line in §5 ("We know what happens. People who win the lottery mostly keep working. And years later, they're happier.") would back up the Kenya beat with the exact scenario of the film.

### 5 · Ending

### 6 · Cheap robotic TTS test

- **Works:** most lines are 3–12 words, subject and verb come early, questions use clear question syntax, the refrain is short and repeatable. It will carry.
- **Breaks:**

  - **Quoted questions** in §5 (`Not "What do you do?" But "What makes you come alive?"`) are read as the narrator asking the viewer. Rewrite as "At parties, nobody asks what you do anymore. They ask what you'd do even if nobody paid you."
