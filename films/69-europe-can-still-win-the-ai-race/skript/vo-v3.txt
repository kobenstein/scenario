Europe can still win the race for artificial intelligence.
Everyone says it has already lost. Too slow. Too small. Too many rules.
And whoever builds this technology gets a say in how it treats you. Which AI reads your contracts. Sits in on your job interview. Says yes or no to your loan.

Right now, almost none of that AI is European.
So how could Europe possibly still win?
Guess how much of the world's computing power for AI sits in the European Union.
About five percent.
And yet: the only machines on Earth that can print the most advanced chips are built in Europe. Then shipped away.
Many of the people who build AI are trained in Europe. Then hired away.
And the European Union has more people than the United States.
So Europe isn't too small.
It's in twenty-seven pieces.
Twenty-seven countries, twenty-seven sets of rules.
And the plan to put them together is already on the table. This winter, the first big decisions are due.

Those chip machines come from a single company, in a Dutch town.
Each is the size of a bus. Shipping one fills three cargo jets. And almost every advanced AI chip in the world was printed by one.
This year, by its own executive's account, the company has sold absolutely nothing in Europe.
So the strengths are real. They're just not connected.
The plan to connect them isn't new. Mario Draghi, the man who once saved the euro, wrote it down.

A harder place. And today, by one count, only about one in six of his proposals has been fully carried out.
For AI, five parts of the plan matter most. Money. One set of rules. Energy. A lab. And minds.
That last one can't be built. It has to be won.
One test for all five: is it really there, or only on paper?

Money first. Europe isn't short of it.
In the European Union, households keep about ten trillion euros in bank accounts. If you live there, part of that is yours.
A young AI company in Paris or Munich barely sees any of it. So its founders fly to California.
The plan isn't to take anyone's savings. It's to make investing them in Europe easier.
Most of it isn't law yet.
Critics warn: push savers into markets, and you push them into risk.
But the money going into Europe's start-ups is up by more than half this year.
Europe now has its own fund, so its biggest start-ups can grow without leaving.
One of its first bets: Mistral, Europe's best-known AI company.
A year earlier, Mistral's lead investor was the company that builds those chip machines. That's what connected looks like.
So, the money: halfway there.

But why does it matter where a company like that lives?

A client state. He means the systems a country cannot run without: its defence, its public services.
And a client doesn't get a say.
So Europe needs companies that can grow up at home. Money alone won't do it.

Because a company that wants to grow across Europe runs into twenty-seven different company laws.
The plan has a fix: a new kind of company, valid in all twenty-seven countries. Set up online in two days, for under a hundred euros.
But it's held up in the European Parliament. Unions fear companies could use it to get around workers' rights.
A fair worry. And even if it's solved, the first such company isn't expected before twenty twenty-eight.
So that one set of rules is still on paper.

None of it runs without electricity. AI data centres need enormous amounts.
And for heavy industry, electricity in the European Union costs more than twice as much as in America.
But not everywhere. Sweden's north has cheap hydropower. France has its reactors. So the idea: build the biggest data centres where the power is.

And even if all of them get built, one Brussels think tank says, Europe's five percent barely moves.
So this part is halfway there, too. One thing is settled: Europe won't have the most computers.

Europe does need enough computers of its own. Enough for what?
For one shared lab, building the most advanced AI.
Europe has done this before. After the war, its countries pooled their money for one physics lab. The World Wide Web was invented there.
It's called CERN. And Europe's top official, Ursula von der Leyen, has promised a CERN for AI.
So far, that promise is a pilot worth about a hundred million euros.
In November, she named the price of really catching up:

At least five hundred billion euros. Her estimate. Not a budget.
A name and a pilot. The lab, too, is still on paper.

And a lab is an empty building without the fifth part. The one that can't be built: minds.
Picture a researcher in Paris, just finished her doctorate. Two offers. One keeps her in Europe. The other, in America, can pay up to seventy percent more. Before stock options.
Many take it.
Part of Europe's answer: stock options that work the same in every country, taxed only when you sell.
But look where that answer sits. Inside that new Europe-wide company. The one that's still on paper.
And yet, something has shifted. Researchers leaving the European Union for America are down by almost half in two years.
A few are even moving the other way.
So the fifth part stays open. It can't be passed in Brussels. It has to be won, one person at a time.

So suppose all five come together. What does Europe win?
Not the biggest chatbot. But a second race is still open: putting AI to work.
One satellite maker is using it to cut its development time.
And more than half of Europe's biggest companies are over a hundred years old. Much of what they know has never been on the internet. No chatbot has read it.

No single company has enough data. Together, they could.
The other prize is a say in the rules. A say goes to whoever builds what others can't do without. Like those chip machines.
But it only counts if Europe speaks with one voice. Today, the Dutch government decides who gets the machines, Washington has a say, and China is building its own.
How to get to one voice is an open argument: more shared money, or fewer rules.
Who settles it? Governments. The European Parliament. Investors. And at the next election, voters.

So that's where the plan stands: two parts halfway there, two on paper, one still to be won.
None is finished. But every one of them has started.
