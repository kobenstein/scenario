### 1 · The promise (0:00–1:07) · Hook

Superintelligence comes with a promise. It will give you your time back.
Bill Gates is already picturing it.
CLIP: Bill Gates, "The purpose of life is not just to do jobs. So if you eventually get a society where you only have to work three days a week or something, that's probably okay."
More of your life back. That's the deal on the table.

Cut to ORIGINAL FOOTAGE (Gates, What Now? with Trevor Noah) or the quote card with the small hourglass glowing in its corner. But you've heard this promise before. The washing machine made it. Email made it. The phone in your pocket made it.
So why does everyone you know feel busier than ever?

In the next few minutes: when technology really gave people their time, when it didn't, and the one thing that decides whether you get yours this time.

### 2 · Some of them came true (1:07–1:50)

No. Some of them came true.
In Germany, the working year has been cut by more than half since your great-great-grandparents' day. Most rich countries look similar.

PICTURE: The frozen sand starts falling again. Graphic: a long bar of working hours in an old factory ledger, half of it crumbles away into sand; a few more country bars beside it do the same. (The only history sentence before minute 2.)

And it still happens today, in the simplest way. Wherever there's no tap, women and girls spend about two hundred million hours every day carrying water. When the pipe arrives, those hours turn into school. Into work.
In your home, that number is zero. You don't even think about it.
That's what time looks like when it truly arrives. It's so complete, you forget it was ever missing.
So if it worked before, why doesn't it feel that way?

PICTURE: A girl on a long dusty path with a water canister. The water sloshing in the canister turns into sand pouring into the hourglass, and this one time the sand lands in her open hand: she runs, a school bag now on her back, into a classroom. Match cut: water from a kitchen tap in your home, a hand fills a glass without looking. Close on the tap: one last drip falls and becomes the next grain in the hourglass.

### 3 · The hour that disappeared (1:50–3:15)

Because the time didn't vanish. It went somewhere else.
Take the washing machine. It made laundry far easier. We washed more often. We owned more clothes. We wanted them cleaner. The historian Ruth Schwartz Cowan showed how our standards rose with every new machine. Later studies found that cooking and cleaning really did shrink. But shopping, driving and childcare grew.
The work didn't disappear. It moved.

PICTURE: The hourglass sand pours into a laundry basket that keeps growing; a washing machine drum spins, shirts multiply on the line. Explainer graphic: icons for cooking and cleaning shrink, icons for a shopping bag, car keys and a school bag grow into the freed space. The sand visibly flows from one pile to the other (fair: moved, not lost).

Then came email. A letter used to take days. Now it takes a second. So did we write less? We wrote far more. By Microsoft's own count, people using its office software get interrupted about every two minutes. Microsoft even has a name for it. The infinite workday.

PICTURE: A sealed letter travels by coach over hills for days; it folds into a flat envelope icon and lands in an inbox. The unread counter spins up; notification dots pop in like rain. Caption: "Microsoft Work Trend Index 2025 (company data)". An office clock whose hands never stop, the window behind it going from dawn to night and back.

Back in nineteen seventy, the economist Staffan Linder spotted the pattern. The more valuable an hour becomes, the more we cram into it. Even our free time starts to feel rushed. Make something easier, and we simply do more of it.
Every time, the hour was handed over. And every time, the sand slipped away before it reached you.
But this machine is different. It doesn't just make work easier. It can do the work. So this time, the hour reaches you. Right?

PICTURE: One hour as a glass block; icons (meetings, errands, a gym bag, a beach chair with a phone on it) get stuffed in until the block bulges. Back to the hourglass: a hairline crack in the glass, the sand leaking out through it before it reaches the bottom. On "this machine is different", the glowing hands from the first shot return and set down a new, brighter hourglass, no crack, in front of you. Hold on it, hopeful, for "Right?"

### 4 · Not so far (3:15–4:15)

Not so far. [pause]
Remember that company? The researchers were from UC Berkeley. Artificial intelligence did take tasks off people's plates. And people filled the space with new ones, even work that used to belong to other roles. Work crept into the evenings. Some people kept several artificial intelligence assistants running at once, like a juggler adding one more ball.
Linder saw this coming. And artificial intelligence can make every hour worth more than ever.

PICTURE: The new hourglass: a hairline crack appears after all. On a desk, glowing hands lift task cards away; new cards drop in at once, some in other colours (other roles). The same desk at night, window lit. Several assistant windows float above a person's hands like juggling balls; one more is tossed in. Caption: "UC Berkeley / Harvard Business Review, Feb 2026, early findings". On "Linder", the bulging glass hour-block from station 3 returns, now glowing brighter and filling faster.

Even the people at the very top feel it. Demis Hassabis, who leads Google DeepMind, has described having dinner with his family, then starting a second workday that runs deep into the night.
I don't say that to mock him. It's the most human detail in this whole story. The people building the greatest time machine ever made don't have time either.
So maybe it's just us. Maybe people don't really want free time?

PICTURE: A family dinner table, warm light, plates being cleared; the light dims, a single laptop opens in the dark kitchen, a clock's hands sweep toward midnight. Gentle, no irony. Final shot: a person looks at their own reflection in the hourglass glass. CHECK: the Hassabis detail (no clock time spoken).

### 5 · The town that already lived the future (4:15–5:50) · heart

To find out, we have to go to one small town in Michigan. Battle Creek. [pause]
December, nineteen thirty. The Great Depression is crushing America. And W. K. Kellogg, the cereal maker, does something nobody expects. He cuts the workday at his factory from eight hours to six. Partly so he can hire more people.
So what does a town do with two extra hours, every single day?

PICTURE: The reflection in the glass dissolves into an aerial view: a small Michigan town in winter, smoke from a cereal factory, a river curling past. Caption: "Battle Creek, Michigan · 1 December 1930". Factory clock: two hour segments lift out of the dial like slices and float out through the factory window, golden.

Decades later, the historian Benjamin Hunnicutt went and asked the people who lived it. What they remembered wasn't output. It was life. [pause]
Parents spent more time with their children. More time with neighbors. More time at the library. People planted gardens and canned what they grew. They sewed. They made ice cream. In the evenings, there were ball games. There was hunting, and fishing in the river. Some kept a little farm on the side.

PICTURE: The two golden hour slices fall as sand, and this time hands catch it everywhere. Quick, warm, one image per sentence: a parent reading with a child on a porch; neighbors talking over a fence; a library window lit at dusk; hands pressing seeds into soil; mason jars glowing on a shelf; a sewing machine; a hand-cranked ice cream churn; a ball game at sunset; a fishing line over the river; a small vegetable plot behind a house.

Picture a summer evening there. The shift is over, and it's still light. A father walks down to the river with a fishing rod and his kid. On a porch, someone is cranking the ice cream churn. At the ball field, the game is already starting. [pause]
That's the promise on the table today. Battle Creek lived it almost a century ago.
So why doesn't anyone in Battle Creek work six hours today?

PICTURE: Slow down. One continuous, unhurried shot (the only calm one so far): long summer light; a father and child walk past the factory gate toward the river, rods over their shoulders; the camera drifts past a porch where an ice cream churn turns, then over to the ball field, where the first pitch is thrown. (A composed scene, built only from Hunnicutt's list.) A mason jar on a windowsill holds sand that slowly turns into seeds. On the last line: the same street today, empty porches, cars leaving early.

### 6 · Why it disappeared (5:50–7:00) · the turn, 60 % reveal

Not because people stopped wanting it.
First came the war, and the factory went back to eight hours. After the war, each department got to vote for itself. And one by one, for a bit more pay and under real pressure, they chose the longer shift. The last six-hour workers held on until nineteen eighty-five. Then they were told: switch to longer days, or the company might leave Battle Creek.

PICTURE: The factory clock again: the two golden slices slide back into the dial. Raised hands in a hall, ballot slips; along the factory front, windows switch one by one from day light to night-shift light. Last shot: a small group of workers at a gate, a typed notice pinned beside them. Caption: "1985".

Notice what's missing from this story. There was no villain. No single day when someone took the time away. It slipped, one vote, one shift at a time. [pause]
And nobody decided to keep it.

PICTURE: An empty workbench. The hourglass stands on it alone, no hands anywhere near; the sand runs out grain by grain, each grain a tiny raised hand or a punched time card. Hold 2 s in silence.

Think about the time we did keep. The weekend. The eight-hour day. They didn't stay because of a machine. They stayed because people decided. A law. A contract. A factory owner like Henry Ford choosing a five-day week.
The time that slipped away? Nobody ever wrote it down.
So what would a decision like that look like for superintelligence?

PICTURE: A Saturday and Sunday on a calendar, a clock at five p.m., a factory gate: each has a signed sheet of paper pinned to it (a law, a contract, a notice). Then the laundry basket, the inbox, the lunch laptop: no paper on them. Finally a blank sheet of paper lies next to the new glowing hourglass. A pen enters frame.

### 7 · The decision for the new machine (7:00–9:20) · solutions, longest part

It starts where you work.
A few years ago, dozens of companies in Britain tried a four-day week. A year later, almost nine in ten were still doing it. About half had made it permanent.
Now carry that idea into the age of superintelligence. When machines take over part of the work, part of what they save comes back to people as hours, not only as profit. Call it a time dividend.
It's a proposal. No country has made it law yet. But notice what it doesn't do. It takes nothing anyone already has. It simply agrees where some of the new time will go.

PICTURE: The pen writes the first line on the sheet. An office calendar: the Friday tile flips over and becomes a park bench in sunlight. Graphic: a field of small office lights (UK pilot 2022); a year later almost all are still lit, about half glow gold (permanent). Caption: "UK four-day week pilot, Autonomy 2024". Time dividend: work flows into a soft-light machine; what comes out splits in two streams, coins on one side, sand on the other, and the sand flows back into people's hands. The coins already on the table stay untouched. Label: "proposal".

It won't fit every job the same way. A hospital can't just close on Fridays. But a rule doesn't have to be perfect to change what's normal.

PICTURE: A hospital corridor at night, a nurse walking past lit rooms (the honest limit). The corridor's light spills onto the sheet of paper, where a second line gets written.

Then there's all of us, together. France gave workers a right to disconnect. After hours, you don't have to answer. Belgium and Australia followed. Spain, Portugal and Ireland have rules of their own.
That's the real shift. Your evening stops being a favor you have to ask for. It's simply yours.

PICTURE: Evening dinner table; a phone lights up with a work message and slides face-down by itself. Map: France lights up, then Belgium, Australia, then Spain, Portugal, Ireland. Back at the table, the evening goes on: someone pours a drink, a kid climbs onto a lap. CHECK: Portugal year (not spoken).

The economist John Maynard Keynes once predicted we'd grow rich enough to work fifteen hours a week. He was right about the riches. Mostly, we took the money, not the time. This time, we can ask out loud: more money, or more time?
So who decides? The companies rolling out these systems. The governments writing the rules. And everyone who votes, negotiates or speaks up at work.

PICTURE: A balance scale: a stack of coins on one side, the hourglass on the other; the coins have always won, the scale stuck. Caption: "J. M. Keynes, 1930". Three quick shots for responsibility: a boardroom table, a parliament chamber, a hand dropping a ballot. The scale starts to level.

And then there's you. A study across four countries found that people who spent money to buy themselves time were happier. Yet when researchers handed people unexpected cash, only two in a hundred spent it on time. We are strangely bad at valuing our own hours.
So here's one small step. The first hour artificial intelligence saves you, don't fill it right away. Notice it. Guard it. Let it stay empty for a moment.
But there's one thing all these rules have in common. And it isn't what they say. It's when they came.

PICTURE: Hands receiving a banknote; a crowd of a hundred small figures, almost all walk into shops, two sit down on a park bench. Caption: "Whillans et al., PNAS 2017". Your desk: a calendar slot glows empty and nobody fills it; your hand closes a lid on the hourglass. On "when they came": the signed papers from station 6 (weekend, contract, right to disconnect) fly onto a timeline.

### 8 · The hour that arrives (9:20–10:33) · big answer

The factories came first. The weekend came generations later. Email filled our evenings for years before France wrote down a right to switch off. Again and again, the machine came first, and the rule came after. By then, the hours were already gone. [pause]
Time never arrives on its own. It arrives when we decide, ahead of time, that it belongs to us.
And this time, for the first time, we can. Before the machine hands out the time. Not after.

PICTURE: Timeline graphic: a factory icon, then a long empty stretch of spilled sand, then the paper "weekend" lands, far too late. An envelope icon, a stretch of spilled sand, then the paper "right to disconnect" lands, late again. Hold on the spilled sand. Then the glowing hands from the very first shot return, about to hand over the new hourglass, and this time the signed sheet of paper is already lying underneath, and your open hand rests on it, before anything is handed out. CHECK: factories before the weekend, email before 2017 (general knowledge, not on the fact sheet).

Superintelligence could give us more time than any machine before it. And we need it. In America, time with friends has been shrinking for twenty years. Trips into nature have been falling since the nineteen-eighties.

PICTURE: Two quiet graphic lines sink gently: an empty chair at a café table among friends; a trail sign in a forest with fewer and fewer footprints. Captions: "American Time Use Survey 2003–2023", "Pergams & Zaradic, PNAS 2008".

So picture the hour arriving. A garden, like the ones in Battle Creek. A long dinner with the people you love. A child by the river. A walk through the woods with nowhere to be.
That's what the time is for.

PICTURE: The sand lands in your hand and turns to soil, a seedling pushes up: a garden (echo of Battle Creek, today, anywhere). A long table outdoors, candles, laughter. A child crouching at the river's edge, the same summer light as the Battle Creek evening. A forest path, slow steps, birdsong.

The machine can give you the hour. [pause]
Only we can decide to keep it.

PICTURE: The hourglass stands on a windowsill, the sand at rest in the bottom bulb, sunlight passing through it. Outside the window, the garden.
