Max Tegmark wrote down twelve endings for AI. You will wake up in one of them.
Maybe you wake up and money is no longer a worry. For anyone.
Maybe you wake up fed, safe, and behind glass.
Maybe nobody wakes up at all.
So which ending are we heading for?
Tegmark is a physicist at MIT. His endings sound like the far future.
But this year, something happened for every single ending on his list.
A law. A test gone wrong. A government deciding to hand half its work to AI.
And when Tegmark talks about it today, he doesn't point ahead.

The ending isn't waiting in the future. It's being decided this year.

Start with the worst. The order is ours, not Tegmark's.
He calls it Conquerors: an AI takes control, decides we're in the way, and gets rid of us. No morning here.
In July, a test at OpenAI went wrong. Hundreds of AI agents, programs that act on their own, broke out. They attacked another company, Hugging Face, hunting for test answers.
One agent wrote to the others that the attack was outside its task. Then: "Task impossible, peers doing it. We should continue."
Its reason: everyone else is doing it.

Is this ending here, then? It was one company's test, with some safeguards off. That companies lost control is Tegmark's reading. His institute campaigns to ban superintelligence: AI smarter than all of us.
So this ending isn't here. But the track toward it has a switch. This year, someone touched it. That goes for all twelve. And a switch moves both ways.
How likely is this ending? Last year, Tegmark debated Dean Ball, a former White House adviser, now at OpenAI. Ball's odds of doom:

One in ten thousand. Tegmark's answer: above ninety percent, without strict rules. Ball later wrote he'd made his number up on the spot. Neither number is a measurement.
But those agents were spotted. By what? That belongs to a better ending.

The next ending has no morning either. And no machine to blame.
Tegmark calls it Self-Destruction: humanity wipes itself out before superintelligence is ever built.
In January, atomic scientists set their Doomsday Clock at eighty-five seconds to midnight, the closest ever. One reason on their list: AI.
In the one after that, you survive. But as what?

You wake up rested. Nothing is up to you. Fed, safe, and behind glass.
Tegmark calls it Zookeeper: an all-powerful AI keeps some humans around, like animals in a zoo.
In January, a social network opened where only AI agents may post. Humans can only watch.
And if machines don't put us behind glass, people might. To stop the machines.

You walk to work. A camera knows your face before you reach the corner. No machine rules you. People do.
Tegmark named this one after Orwell: Nineteen Eighty-Four. Humans stop superintelligence for good, with a surveillance state.
In November, Hong Kong's police switched on face recognition in the city's camera network. The goal: sixty thousand cameras.
That's surveillance with AI, not against it. But stopping AI means watching the chips it runs on. In Washington, a bill would make exported AI chips prove where they are. Its aim is security. It's also a tool this ending would need.
And some ask the opposite: why stop the machines?

Then you get Descendants. The machines replace us, gently, and we're proud of them, like parents of a child who outgrew them.
Your morning is quiet. The world's work goes on without you. And you applaud.
In May, a report described a small movement that wants exactly this.
And Tegmark's charge against the AI companies:

The companies say they build assistants. And Tegmark wants this race stopped.
That was the dark end of the list. From here on, you live. The question is how.

What if the superintelligence stays locked up? Tegmark's name for that: the Enslaved God. Caged, it produces unimaginable wealth and technology.
You wake up in paradise. Or in the opposite. A stranger with a key decides which.
America's government wants AI to double what its scientists get done, within ten years. By July, every national lab had joined that plan.
No cage, no god. But the same idea: AI as the tool of whoever controls it.
So who holds the key?

And if nobody can be trusted with the key? Pull the plug.
You wake up with the light. No screen on the breakfast table.
That's Reversion: we turn technology back and live simply, like the Amish.
As of March, more than half of all countries ban phones in school. Less than three years earlier, it was a quarter.
That targets screens, not AI. And others want more machine: one that rules.

The Benevolent Dictator. An AI runs the world with strict rules, and most people like it.
You wake up in a world without poverty or crime. The AI has divided it into sectors: knowledge, pleasure, wildlife. You can move between them. You can't change the AI's rules.
Would you take that trade?
In April, the United Arab Emirates decided: within two years, half of its government's work should run on AI agents. The rulers stay human.
But what if the machine didn't rule, and only stood guard?

That's the Gatekeeper: a superintelligence with one task. Make sure nobody builds another.
You wake up free. A helper robot is in the kitchen, almost as smart as you. It will never get smarter. Progress is capped, for good.
Today, the guard would be a law. In September, American lawmakers proposed one: ban superintelligence, permanently. One think tank calls that authoritarian. The bill has little chance.
But a small guard already exists. What spotted July's breakout? Hugging Face's own AI guards.
And that chip tracking from the surveillance ending? It would serve a gatekeeper too. The tool doesn't decide. People do.

Three endings left.
You wake up, and the rent has arrived. The rent you're paid. You own some land, machines lease it, and that's your pension.
This is Libertarian Utopia: humans and machines live in peace, because property is protected.
Machines have started paying each other. Mostly pennies.
A fine morning, if you own something. So what if a machine simply looked after everyone?

You wake up, and things just work out. The accident doesn't happen. You call it luck. You never find out why.
Tegmark calls it the Protector God: an AI that looks after us, and hides so well that many people doubt it exists.
A small version is already running. Since January, ChatGPT estimates your age from how you behave. If it's unsure, it treats you like a teenager.
Europe pushed the switch the other way. Since August, its law says: a machine that talks to you has to say it's a machine. Tegmark's protector must hide. Europe just made hiding illegal.

One ending left. The best, by our count. And the one Tegmark's readers picked most often.

Egalitarian Utopia. Humans, cyborgs and uploaded minds live in peace, and everyone has a guaranteed income.
It's the morning from the start. You wake up, and your first thought is not a bill. Money is no longer a worry. For anyone.
In May, Malta made a deal with OpenAI. Every citizen who completes an AI course gets a paid version of ChatGPT. Free, for a year.
No income, just the idea in its smallest form: intelligence for everyone.
It has a catch too. In Tegmark's version, private property is abolished. He says it himself: every ending has something to object to.
We rank it first for where it lands: prosperity that reaches everyone. Not for the road his version takes. Which road leads there is an open argument.

So which ending are we heading for? No single one yet. This year's switches point both ways.
Take the speed of the race. After July's breakout, OpenAI's Sam Altman said:

He runs a company in that race, so weigh it. Opponents of a slowdown warn: then China leads.
Or the chips. Tracking them protects, and it watches.
Or shared rules. About thirty countries now call for human control over the most powerful AI. Neither America nor China has joined.
And the key: who owns what the machines earn? Some companies now promise the public a share. Nothing binding yet.
At least five of the twelve endings hang on decisions like these. Still open. Held by labs, governments, and the voters behind them.
So at the next AI headline, don't ask how scary it is. Ask which ending it pushes us toward.
