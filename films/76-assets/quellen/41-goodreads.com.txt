# What Should My Children Do?: A Human's Guide to the Age of AI

 __

### 

Daniel Susskind

4.10

115 ratings18 reviews

Want to Read

 __

__

Rate this book

**From the author of _Growth_ , one of Barack Obama’s favorite books of 2024, a guide for helping the next generation through an educational world, and a job market, forever changed by AI – as well as for anyone navigating the upheaval in today’s workplace**  
  
We live at an extraordinary technological moment. Every day, it seems, we hear about machines taking on tasks that we thought only human beings alone would ever making medical diagnoses and composing jokes, drafting legal arguments and designing beautiful buildings, writing lines of code and even forming relationships. The leaders of the largest AI companies now claim that, within a few years, we will build an AI that can outperform us at every task that we do.  
  
For the last fifteen years, Prof. Daniel Susskind has been exploring the impact of technology on work. Recent developments in AI – ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Grok – are astonishing, but also best thought of as a remarkable new chapter in this far longer story.  
  
Still, the most common question people have remains–what should my children do?  
  
_What Should my Children Do?_ is an optimistic and hopeful guide for parents thinking about how to help their families flourish in this age of astonishing labor market shifts. It is also for anyone thinking about their career, asking how to navigate the turmoil that is underway in the working world, as well as for business and political leaders navigating the same upheaval.  
  
With his deep knowledge of AI and how the world of work is changing, Prof. Susskind offers an evidence-based look at the problem and a practical path forward.

November 20, 2026

“Since AI is going to take most of our jobs, how can I best prepare my kids for their future?”  
  
I have thought about this question a lot over the past year as I have done a deep dive into artificial intelligence. Very few books address it in more than a sentence or two, yet it matters enormously to parents, educators, and society at large. I am happy to say that Daniel Susskind’s What Should My Children Do? A Human’s Guide to the Age of AI did not disappoint.  
  
There is much to say about this book, and I don’t agree with all of it, but I think most parents and educators should read it so they can better prepare the next generation for the wild future that is in store for them.   
  
Here are the three most helpful lessons I took away.  
  
1\. Prepare your kids to flourish with AI, and to flourish without it.  
  
This may seem obvious, but I think most of us lean too far in one direction. Before reading this book, I kept my kids away from AI as much as possible because I feared its negative side effects. Susskind points out, however, that there are great ways to teach kids to use AI to learn and study better. When the calculator was invented, many feared students would become overly reliant on it. The best classroom solution was to give some tests with calculators and some without, because students needed to learn both. It would be great if teachers did the same with AI, testing kids both with it and without it to prepare them for the future.  
  
2\. Don’t try to compete with AI. Use it.  
  
We have no idea which jobs will exist in twenty years, and we do real harm by pretending we do. Rather than hunting for the things AI isn’t good at right now and steering our kids toward those careers, we should encourage them to pursue their interests and see where AI intersects with them. Susskind writes, “Preparing the next generation for the future of work by telling them to double down on whatever AI cannot yet do, to keep on retreating to the limits in the current capabilities of technology, is a mistake. Not only are those gaps getting smaller but, again, they are changing in ways that are hard for anyone, however thoughtful, to anticipate” (p. 77). He notes that coding was once considered the best career to prepare young people for the future, until tools like ChatGPT and Claude shocked the world with how well they can code. AI is part of the future, so let’s help our kids imagine what their work could look like alongside it rather than fighting against it.  
  
3\. We may still prefer humans in some areas, even when AI can do the job better.  
  
Many people reassure themselves that their job is safe because “people will always want a human for this.” That isn’t true in most cases because what we really want is a job to be done quickly and accurately. If an AI lawyer, financial planner, driver can get us what we need more effecitvely than humans, we will choose the AI bots. But Susskind identifies some areas where we may still desire humans.  
• Aesthetics and art. People may still want human beings involved in creative work rather than machines alone.  
• Achievement. In sports and games, we don’t care to watch a robot run fast; we want to see humans do it.  
• Empathy. At the end of life, we may still want human nurses at our bedside rather than AI.  
• Ethical decisions. We may want humans, not AI, making moral judgments. As Susskind puts it, “You should pay attention to those parts of the working world where a human process is the thing that we value, where people really do care that other human beings remain in the loop” (p. 173).  
  
While no one can accurately predict the future, Susskind does a great job helping guide us into the unknown.

November 22, 2026

Welp. I begged my public library to get this book so I could read it, but now that I’ve read it, I regret their purchase. AI probably wrote the whole thing!  
  
Susskind seemed like he was speaking my language in the very beginning by showing the harm that social media and distraction have done to the minds of youth since 2009. He showed clear evidence of a great mental health decline and academic aptitude loss. I was on board with his stressing the importance of students having a strong grasp of basic skills: numeracy, literacy, and critical thinking.   
  
But from there, he lost me. No mention of data centers’ effects on nearby community utilities, no mention of environmental impact. He’s British and his political leanings make an appearance on page 181.  
  
I was reading this as a parent first, as a speculative citizen of the 21st century second. For this reason, the book left me frustrated. I am still not sure what my children should do. Be electricians, midwives, barbers, teachers, elder care workers? Adjust their prissy little expectations of what a “good career” looks like? Will there be any paid jobs left for humans that don’t involve danger and mess and chaos? Susskind makes very few speculations about how their world will really be, just encourages embracing AI instead of fighting it, and repeatedly admits there is a lot we don’t know.   
  
At the end of this book, I’d been messed with enough to wonder, what is the point of us imperfect humans after all? If AGI is going to be so superior to our efforts, why would we even bother living in the same sphere? This isn’t my usual outlook!! So, not recommended.

Daniel Susskind is an evangelist for AI, extolling the extraordinary ways it is helping us learn and create. However, he isn't blind to its limitations and risks. One of the approaches he advocates in this book, which is not just for your children but for all of us, is to learn non-AI and AI methods of doing things.  
  
Veering towards a brusque tone - you should be for AI. If you're not, there's something wrong with you - it never stays into causing offence. It is best described as a call to arms and an urging to get used to AI because it isn't going away, despite opposition to data centres, and issue, incidentally, which is barely discussed on these pages.   
  
Susskind believes opponents of AI are wasting their time. He deftly explains his case in this book.
