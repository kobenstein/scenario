Three years ago, his reasoning was familiar. New technology had always cost jobs, and created new ones. On balance, he was an optimist.
His new essay says: there will be some new jobs. But without the right policies, far fewer than exist today.
What changed is his picture of the machine. This technology, he now says, is different from every technology before it. Because it exceeds human intelligence.
Earlier machines took over tasks, and people moved on to work the machines couldn't do. That is what Bill Gates saw: this time, the machine can follow them.
He says he would love to be convinced that he's wrong about the job market. But he isn't. He has put his reputation on it.
So where does it begin? His warning comes with a timeline. Three stops. The first is now.

For Gates, the first work to go is work at a screen. He names it: phone support. Telemarketing. Accounting. These systems, he says, do that work cheaper, faster and more accurately.
And for one group, this stop is no forecast. It shows up in payroll data.
People in their early twenties, looking for a first job. In the jobs most exposed to artificial intelligence, their employment now trails less exposed jobs by about nineteen percent. A year earlier, the gap was smaller.
If that's you, this stop isn't ahead of you. You're standing in it.

Before the last stop on his timeline, though, a fair question. Is he even right?

Start with who's talking. Bill Gates co-founded Microsoft, the biggest partner of the company behind ChatGPT. So he is warning about a technology his old company profits from. That makes the warning harder to wave away. Though a skeptic would add: strict rules often suit the biggest players.

Twenty years of winners and losers. If you're in the middle of your working life, that's not a transition. That's the rest of it.
Remember his sentence: without the right policies. So which policies? His first is about money.
Today, he argues, the tax system pushes the wrong way. Hire a person, and the company pays taxes on the wage. Replace that person with software, and those taxes disappear.
His fix: tax the machine's work. A company that replaces people with software or robots pays. The money goes into retraining and a safety net.
The artificial intelligence researcher Oren Etzioni says Gates has the diagnosis right, but the prescription wrong, at least in some places. Taxing tokens, the small units these systems work in, is like taxing keystrokes on a typewriter, he says. It counts how hard the machine works. Not whether anyone lost a job.
And even a perfect tax leaves a gap. Money can replace a paycheck. It can't replace being needed.
That's where his second idea comes in. The list.

Human Reserved. In his essay, Gates puts it like this: as artificial intelligence and robots improve, we'll set aside certain things for only people to do.
The candidates named so far: looking after children. Sitting on a jury. Making art. And in teaching, a mix: a person stays in charge, and uses the machine as a tool.
Take the jury. Imagine the verdict is about you. A machine might weigh the evidence faster. Would you still want people to decide?
Human Reserved would turn an answer like that into a rule.
How long could such a list get? Take a guess.
His own ceiling: in a very extreme form, forty percent of jobs could be reserved for humans, at first. And that, he says, is as high as he can get.
So even at its most generous, most jobs would not be on it.
Which ones would? This October, on television, Gates named the task.

Decide which jobs. And by his own account, the hardest parts of that are open. Who decides what belongs on the list. By which criteria. And how to stop companies from replacing people anyway.
Critics add: a line like this is hard to draw, and easy to lobby. And reserved doesn't mean well paid. Care work and teaching often aren't.
So the list is still an idea. A few candidates. Not one line of it is written.
And still: look at what this idea does to the question. Until now, which jobs stay human was rarely a decision. It was a price. If a machine could do the work for less, the work went to the machine. A human job was whatever was left over.
Human Reserved turns that around. Human work would no longer be what's left over. It would be chosen. In advance. And a rule holds, whatever the price.
Try a line yourself. Which work would you put on it? And, harder: what is your reason? Because the reason is exactly what's missing.

Now look past those twenty years. At the far end, Gates sees abundance: cheaper homes, cheaper food, because most goods and services would no longer depend on human labor.
And one task that stays with people. Humanity, he says, will have to find a way to organize, and to have purpose.
That task starts with the list. And an unwritten list doesn't stay unwritten. It gets written by whoever speaks up. Companies. Lawmakers. Or the people whose work is on it.
