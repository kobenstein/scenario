Bill Gates changed his mind about artificial intelligence and jobs.
Three years ago, he called the risks real, but manageable.
Now he warns of far fewer jobs than exist today.
No job, he says, will be left untouched.
Jobs at a screen. Jobs done by hand. Whatever your job is, he means yours too.
So what did Bill Gates see?
His own words: "This is crazy. This is insane."
He's shocked, he says, to be one of the first saying it. And deafened by the silence.
But he doesn't want to stop the machines.
He wants certain work set aside. For people only. Even where a machine could do it.
Think of it as a list. He calls it Human Reserved.
Your job could be on it. Or left off.
The list hasn't been written yet.

What changed is his picture of the machine. Just weeks ago, he said why this technology is different from past ones. It exceeds human intelligence.
Earlier machines took over tasks, and people moved on to work the machines couldn't do. That's why he called the risks manageable.
This time, wherever people move, the machine can follow. That's what Bill Gates saw.
He says he'd love to be convinced he's wrong about the job market. But he's not convinced. He has put his reputation on it.
So where does it begin? His warning comes with a timeline. Three stops. The last is the one that worries him most. The first is now.

For Gates, the first work to go is work at a screen. He names it. Phone sales. Phone support. Accounting. Artificial intelligence, he says, does that work cheaper, faster and more accurately.
For one group, this stop is already in the data. Young people, looking for their first job.
In the jobs most exposed to artificial intelligence, their employment has fallen about nineteen percent behind people the same age in less exposed jobs. And the gap is still growing.
If that's you, this stop isn't ahead of you. You're standing in it.
But the same data shows something else. Where the technology works alongside people, instead of replacing them, employment holds steady, or grows. So the machine alone doesn't decide. How it's used does.
So the first stop is the screen. Which sounds like good news for anyone who works with their hands.

He isn't describing the weather. He's describing a decision. Machines like these are built, bought and switched on by people. Gates doesn't expect a halt. But how fast they come, and where, is a choice.
Before the last stop on his timeline, though, a fair question. Is he even right?

Start with who's talking. Bill Gates co-founded Microsoft, one of the biggest shareholders in the company behind ChatGPT. So he is warning about a technology his old company profits from. That makes the warning harder to wave away. Though a skeptic would add: strict rules often suit the biggest players.
Then the numbers. That nineteen percent gap is about the youngest workers, in the most exposed jobs. Across the whole American job market, researchers at Yale see something else. As of this spring, their verdict: where the job market is weakening, artificial intelligence is probably not the reason. Not yet.
And Gates doesn't claim otherwise. By his own account, his warning is a forecast. Not a finding in today's numbers.

Twenty years of winners and losers. If you're in the middle of your working life, that's not a transition. That's the rest of it.
That stretch is what his plan is for. Remember the warning from the start? Far fewer jobs. His essay adds a condition: without the right policies.
The list is one of them. Another is about money. Today, he argues, the tax system pushes the wrong way. Hire a person, and the company pays taxes on the wage. Buy a robot instead, and it can usually be written off right away.
His fix: tax the machine's work. Tax artificial intelligence and robots by how much they're used. The money goes into retraining and a stronger safety net.
The artificial intelligence researcher Oren Etzioni says Gates has the diagnosis right, but the prescription wrong, at least in some places. Taxing the machine's work, he says, is like taxing keystrokes on a typewriter. It counts how hard the machine works. Not whether anyone lost a job.
And even a perfect tax leaves a gap. Money can replace a paycheck. It can't replace being needed.
That's where his second idea comes in. The list.

Human Reserved. In his words: as artificial intelligence and robots improve, we'll set aside certain things for only people to do.
The candidates named so far: looking after children. Sitting on a jury. Making art. And in teaching, a mix: a person stays in charge, and uses the machine as a tool.
Take the jury. Imagine the verdict is about you. A machine might weigh the evidence faster. Would you still want people to decide?
Human Reserved would turn an answer like that into a rule.
How long could such a list get? Take a guess.
His own ceiling: in a very extreme form, forty percent of jobs could be reserved for humans, at first. And that, he says, is as high as he can get.
So even at its most generous, the list leaves most jobs off. Maybe yours.
Which ones make it? In his essay, Gates asks that himself.

By his own account, those questions are still open. And so is a third: how to keep companies from replacing people anyway.
Critics add: a line like this is hard to draw, and open to lobbying. And reserved doesn't mean well paid. Care work and teaching often aren't.
So the list is unwritten. Hard to draw. And too short for most jobs. Why does it matter, then?
Ask who decides, right now, which work stays human. Mostly, nobody does. The price does. If a machine can do the work for less, the machine gets it. A human job is whatever is left over.
Chances are, that includes yours. No rule ever said a person has to do it. A person has simply been the best deal. So far.
Human Reserved asks something no price can answer. Not: what does it cost? But: why should a person do this?
Try a line yourself. Which work would you put on it? And, harder: what's your reason? That reason is exactly what the list is still missing.

Now look past those twenty years. At the far end, Gates sees abundance. Cheaper homes, cheaper food, because most goods and services would no longer depend on human labor.
And one task stays with people. Humanity, he says, will have to find a way to organize, and to have purpose.
A list like Human Reserved is one way to begin. But a list nobody writes still gets filled in. By price. One cheaper machine at a time.
And so far, Gates says, what he hears is silence. Which means every line is still open.
