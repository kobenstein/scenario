Superintelligence can make you smarter than anyone before you. Or it can think for you.

In Turkey, almost a thousand students took part in a math experiment.

Two groups had the same machine, with one small difference. A third group had none.

While practicing, both machine groups flew.

Then came the exam, without the machine.

One group did worse than students who never had it.

The other lost nothing.

What was the difference? Find it, and you'll know which of those students you're becoming.

In Poland, nineteen very experienced doctors got a new assistant: a machine that marks tiny growths that can turn into cancer.

These weren't beginners. Each of them had done more than two thousand of these exams.

Then researchers looked at the exams they did without the machine.

Before it arrived, they found a growth in about twenty-eight percent of exams.

A few months after it arrived, it was twenty-two.

It's a signal, not proof. But it's a simple one.

What you stop doing, you start to lose. Even as an expert.

Not necessarily.

In early twenty twenty-five, a research lab asked sixteen experienced programmers to do real work on big projects they knew well. Sometimes with artificial intelligence, sometimes without.

Before they started, they expected it to make them faster.

With the tools of that time, it made them slower. They took nineteen percent longer.

And here's the strange part. Afterwards, they still believed it had sped them up.

They felt faster. They were slower.

One small study. But a survey of people at work adds a reason. The more they trusted the machine, the less critical thinking they said they did.

The writer Ted Chiang has a picture for this. Using a chatbot for homework, he says, is like bringing a forklift into the weight room.

The weights go up. It even feels like progress. But your arms stay the same.

So is this simply what happens when machines get smarter than us? Do we always get weaker?

Remember the Go players.

In twenty sixteen, a machine called AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol, one of the world's best.

Later, researchers had a machine grade professional play, move by move, all the way back to nineteen fifty.

For sixty-six years, the line was flat.

Then the machine won. And the line went up.

Players started making moves that had never shown up in recorded professional games. And those new moves went hand in hand with better play.

The machine was stronger than them. And it made them better.

The study shows that it happened, not exactly why. But this is the part I keep coming back to.

Next to a machine, the doctors slipped. Next to a far stronger one, the Go players got better than ever.

So what did the Go players do that the doctors didn't?

It seems they didn't let the machine play for them. They studied it.

The doctors did what their machine was built for. They let it do the looking.

So is that the whole difference? Who does the thinking?

A physics class at Harvard put that to the test.

Researchers built a tutor that followed the rules of good teaching. It didn't hand out answers. It asked questions, and led students one step at a time.

Against a very good class with experienced teachers, the tutor students learned about twice as much. In less time.

The teachers built the tutor themselves. One strong case, not the last word.

And now you know the small difference in Turkey.

The group that lost nothing had a machine built on the same idea. Hints. Never answers.

Their study's title says it plainly: without guardrails, this kind of machine can harm learning.

But most machines you'll use have no such rule. They'll do everything, if you let them.

So what then?

One of the companies building these machines studied exactly that: Anthropic, the maker of Claude.

Fifty-two programmers, most of them early in their careers, learned a new coding tool. Half of them had a machine to help.

That half scored worse on a test afterwards.

Those who just let it write the code finished fastest. But they scored under forty percent.

Those who kept asking why things worked scored sixty-five percent or more.

Same machine. Different habit.

The groups were small. But their conclusion stuck with me: effort, and even getting painfully stuck, is likely part of how you master something.

So here's the rule. When the machine thinks for you, you get weaker. When it makes you think, you get stronger.

The World Bank compared their overall gains to one and a half to two years of normal schooling.

A small pilot. The comparison group got no extra lessons, and the teachers mattered a lot. But look where it points.

This is the part that gives me hope.

For most of history, a personal tutor was something only the rich could have. Now it could cost almost nothing. For every child on Earth.

Let's imagine twenty thirty-five. Twice.

In the first version, answers cost nothing. Everyone has a forklift. Thinking for yourself becomes a luxury for the few. Most people never lift again, and never notice.

In the second version, every machine starts out as a personal trainer. Schools, companies and the makers set it up that way. A generation grows up that asks why before it asks for help. And it becomes the smartest generation there has ever been.

Both cities run on the same machines. The difference is a choice we're making right now.

...

So what can you do, starting tomorrow? And who has to help?

...

Start with you. One habit, in three moves.

First: try before you ask. Make a guess. Write the first line. Get a little stuck.

That struggle isn't wasted time. That's the lift.

Second: ask why. Instead of "write this for me," ask "why does this work?" It seems that's what the Go players did.

Then explain it back in your own words. If you can't, you haven't got it yet.

Third: decide what you're happy to hand over. Mental math. Formatting. The boring parts. Let it carry those.

And decide what you want to keep. Your judgment. Your writing. The thing you want to be truly good at. Those, you lift yourself.

If this sounds like an old worry, it is. In Plato's Phaedrus, a king warns that writing will "create forgetfulness in the learners' souls."

The worry was real. And writing made us smarter anyway. Because we learned to think with it, not let it think for us.

But you can't set up every machine yourself. What about the one a child uses for homework tonight?

The big makers already offer learning modes that guide instead of answer. But they're usually switched off until someone turns them on.

A tutor for every child on Earth, or a forklift for every child on Earth. That's a setting. And these companies can change it tomorrow.

Schools can choose tutors over bans. Remember Harvard. And remember Turkey: the danger wasn't the machine itself. It was a machine without guardrails.

And where a lot is at stake, keep practicing without help. Pilots still practice flying by hand, in the simulator and in the air. Doctors could train the same way.

Go back to the gym.

The machine is standing next to you. It could lift the weight for you. Or it can put one hand on your back, and let you lift.

Superintelligence won't decide whether it makes you smarter or thinks for you. You will. One question at a time.

So the next time you ask it something, ask yourself first: am I lifting? Or is it lifting for me?
