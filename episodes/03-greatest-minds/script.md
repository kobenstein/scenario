# 03 · What If We Could Copy Our Greatest Minds?
Insight: For all of history, progress was limited by how many brilliant minds we had. That limit is ending. What matters now is what we ask them, and whether the world can keep up with their answers.
Misconception: AI = a smarter chatbot that writes your emails.
Through-line image: ONE room full of minds (an unnamed group photo of scientists, drawn in code) that keeps multiplying, and ONE narrow door in front of it, where ideas queue. The film ends with the room full of faces from everywhere and the door wide open.
Words: 1,335 VO (+ 7 clips ≈ 70 s) | Est. runtime: ≈ 10:30 incl. clips and pauses | Solutions share (fork + endings + how + your part): 52 %

## 3 · The oldest limit (1:30–2:40)
VO:
To see why this matters, zoom out. All the way out.
For almost all of human history, there was one simple rule. More people meant more ideas.
Economists looking back a million years found it: places with more people invented faster. [S1]
Because an idea, once found, can be used by everyone.
[pause]
But the best ideas are getting harder to find.
Keeping chips doubling in power now takes more than eighteen times the researchers it did in the early nineteen-seventies. [S2]
We have been running out of geniuses for a long time.
[pause]
Now imagine that limit simply disappears.
Not more scientists. As many as we want.
That's the real meaning of a country of geniuses.
PICTURE: scale shift. A dark map of the Earth across the ages; each tiny light is a person, each spark an invention (fire, wheel, press, bulb). The lights multiply, the sparks follow. Then a staircase: each step is one doubling of a chip, and the crowd of researchers needed to climb it grows step by step until it spills off the frame. Hard cut: the staircase dissolves, the room of lights from the hook fills the frame.
ON SCREEN: "Kremer, 1993"; "Bloom et al., 2020: 18x the researchers".
LOOP: closes "why does it matter?" — opens "is it already happening?" (REVEAL 1, ~25 %)

## 4 · It has already started, quietly (2:40–4:00)
VO:
And small pieces of it already work.
In London, a team of scientists spent about a decade on one question: how do some superbugs share their defences between species? [S6]
They had found the answer. They hadn't published it yet.
Then they gave the same question to an AI system from Google.
Within two days, its top idea matched theirs. [S6]
[pause]
In another lab, an AI system called Robin looked at a common cause of blindness in older people.
It suggested an eye drop already sold in Japan for glaucoma. [S7]
So far, it has only been tested on cells in a dish. It's a lead, not a cure.
But a lead that no human had followed.
[pause]
And a piece of this is already in your pocket.
When OpenAI launched GPT-5, Sam Altman called it a "PhD-level expert" in any area. [S13]
A thousand PhDs, on call, day and night.
Most of us use them to fix our emails.
PICTURE: split timeline: a calendar flipping ten years of pages on one side, a clock moving two days on the other; both stop on the same drawing of a bacterium passing a tiny "key" to another. Then an eye in close-up, pupil opening as a drop falls, a petri dish under a lamp. Then "you" on a bus: from the phone screen, hundreds of tiny figures in lab coats climb out like from a door, look around expectantly; "you" types "rephrase this email more politely"; they sigh in unison.

## 6 · The narrow door (4:45–6:00)
VO:
Thinking was never the only slow step.
A new medicine has to be made. Then tested on cells. Then on animals. Then on people, in stages, for years.

"Usually it takes, like, on average, you know, ten years to go from understanding a target for a disease all the way to a drug that's ready for the market … and most drugs fail along the way." [S11]
VO:
Look at the first drugs designed with AI.
One, for a disease that scars the lungs, is now in its final stage of human testing. [S8]
More than a hundred and seventy are in trials. As of this spring, not one had been approved. [S9]
[pause]
The geniuses are not the bottleneck anymore. The door is.
Amodei puts the question better than anyone.

And what people built, people can change.

## 8 · Two endings (6:25–7:40)
VO:
Let's imagine it's twenty thirty-five. This part is a scenario, not a forecast.
Your mother sits in the waiting room of an eye clinic.
[pause]
In the first future, the room of geniuses has found hundreds of promising leads for her kind of blindness.
They're sitting in a queue.
Trials run the way they always have. The few treatments that make it through are expensive, and reach rich cities first.
Nothing collapsed. We just got a small fraction of what was possible.
She waits.
[pause]
In the second future, it's the same clinic. The same chair.
The drop she takes was a lead in twenty twenty-six.
Robot labs tested thousands of versions in a few months. A trial that once took a decade took a few careful years, with real patients and every safety check.
It costs about what an eye drop costs.
She reads the small print on the wall, out loud, just because she can.

## 9 · How we get there (7:40–10:00)
VO:
Getting there takes three things. None of them is magic, and all three have already worked somewhere.
[pause]
First: labs that move as fast as ideas.
In twenty twenty-three, a lab at Berkeley ran itself.
Robots mixed powders, heated them, measured the result, and an AI chose the next try.
In seventeen days, it made forty-one materials it had never made before. [S14]
Other scientists later questioned how many were truly new. That debate is healthy, and robots still struggle with delicate biology. [S14]
But the idea is spreading: labs that work through the night, so a thousand ideas don't wait for one pair of hands.
[pause]
Second: trials that move at the speed of evidence, without cutting corners on safety.
We've done this before.
In March twenty twenty, British doctors set up one trial across many hospitals, testing several treatments at once. [S15]
Within about a hundred days, it showed that a cheap steroid saved lives. Among the sickest patients on ventilators, it cut deaths by about a third. [S15]
That same year, vaccines went from a genetic sequence to approval in under twelve months. [S16]
They didn't skip the steps. They ran them side by side, with the money committed up front and regulators reading the data as it arrived.
That was an emergency. The work now is to make that speed normal, for diseases that kill quietly.
Health agencies and governments hold that key.
[pause]
Third: geniuses for everyone.
When Google DeepMind solved the shape of proteins, it gave the results away.
CLIP K9 · Demis Hassabis, Google for Developers, 21 May 2026 · youtube.com/watch?v=dgBLVm2L1P4 · 00:17:08–00:17:15
"We just fold everything and then give it to everyone in the world. Who knows how many discoveries will be made from that?" [S12]
VO:
Today, more than three million researchers use it. [S12]
That's what open results can do.
But a market points its geniuses at what pays. Diseases of the poor pay the least.
Even Hassabis says the industry isn't doing enough of this kind of work. [S17]
And Amodei worries about the people who'll be left behind, even after the cures exist.
CLIP K5 · Dario Amodei, Dwarkesh Podcast, 13 Feb 2026 · 01:47:01–01:47:04
"I worry that even if the cures are developed, maybe there's someone in rural Mississippi who doesn't get it." [S5]
VO:
So who decides what a country of geniuses works on?
Right now, a handful of companies. They don't have to be the only ones.
Governments can buy computing time for public science, the way they once built telescopes and particle colliders.
Funders can pay for results that are shared with everyone.
And none of it happens on its own. The labs, the governments, and the voters who choose those governments all have a hand on that door.
PICTURE: (1) the narrow door gets robot arms on both sides, working through a night sky that turns to morning, powders glowing in tiny crucibles; a red margin note "disputed?" appears and stays. (2) a hospital map of Britain; one line connects many hospitals; a single cheap pill bottle lights up; next, a vaccine vial where four stage-boxes that normally sit in a long row slide side by side. (3) a library of glowing protein shapes opening its doors to a world map that lights up city by city; then a balance scale: a pile of coins on one side, a small village clinic on the other; finally a government building pouring light into a public telescope-shaped "compute dome".
ON SCREEN: "A-Lab, Berkeley · Nature 2023"; "RECOVERY trial, UK · 2020"; "first COVID vaccine authorised Dec 2020"; "AlphaFold database · free since 2021".

## 10 · Your part (10:00–10:40)
VO:
You already carry a thousand PhDs in your pocket.
Ask them better questions.
What's known about the illness in your family. What's being tested. What it would take.
If you work in science, health, or government, you're standing at that door. Hold it open.
And ask the people you vote for one simple thing: are we building the door, or just the room?
[pause]
For all of history, progress was limited by how many brilliant minds we had.
That limit is ending.
What matters now is what we ask them.
[pause]
So here's my question for you.
If a million geniuses worked for you tomorrow, what would you ask them first?

Final shot: "you" on the bus again, phone in hand, typing a real question; one tiny figure on the screen looks up and nods.
ON SCREEN: last line as text, then black.
