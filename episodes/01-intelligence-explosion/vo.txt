First: treat near misses like aviation does.
In nineteen seventy-four, a TWA jet flew into a mountain near Washington. Six weeks earlier, a United crew had made the same mistake, and pulled up just in time.
Nobody else heard.
So in nineteen seventy-six, America built a system where pilots report their own mistakes, confidentially and without punishment.
More than two million reports have come in since. Reporting is one reason flying became so safe, alongside better technology and training.
In AI, after Hugging Face disclosed the July breach, OpenAI came forward to say its own models did it.
California now requires frontier AI developers to report critical safety incidents within fifteen days.
The limits: the law's definition is narrow, many near misses fall outside it, and outsiders still haven't seen the full logs of July.
Second: let someone else grade the test.
The July agents tried to cheat on an exam. And that "research intern" milestone? OpenAI graded it itself.
Government testers in Britain and the US now get early access to many frontier models before release.
And in September, OpenAI shelved the launch of a new model after it, in the words of its safety chief, "didn't quite meet the bar."
The lesson: testing works when it's independent and comes before launch, like crash tests for cars.
The limits: it's all voluntary. This autumn, Britain's testers were cut off from new models for seventeen days, after the White House told labs to stop sharing them.
A safety net that one office can switch off isn't a safety net yet.
Third: build the brake while the road is still straight.
We've done this before. In nineteen seventy-four, biologists who had just learned to splice DNA asked their own field to pause certain experiments. And the pause held.
At Asilomar, in California, about a hundred and fifty people, mostly scientists, agreed on rules matching each kind of experiment to a level of containment.
Research went on, and grew into biotech.
For AI, Anthropic writes, a pause would need several labs in several countries to agree, and a way to check they really stopped.
One promising idea: check the chips. They're physical and hard to make, so they can be tracked, and researchers are designing ways for them to prove where they are.
The limits: Asilomar was one small field, not a trillion-dollar race. And chip verification is untested. Researchers say today's chips aren't secure enough for it yet.
Fourth: turn promises into rules.
In September, the biggest AI companies signed a safety pact at the White House: outside auditors, internal monitoring, board oversight.
It's a step. But it's voluntary, with no penalties and no regulator.
Bills in Congress would add audits and reporting with teeth. None has passed.
Critics warn that rules could slow useful AI, or protect giants who can afford compliance. A fair worry, and an argument for rules that are narrow and testable.
