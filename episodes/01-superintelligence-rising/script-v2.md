## 2 · What has started (0:42–2:30)
VO:
If you picture superintelligence, you probably picture a day. Some big announcement, and after that, everything's different.
It's more like a plane on a runway. The wheels haven't left the ground yet. But it keeps picking up speed, and the speed comes from AI helping to build better AI. [S1]
Some numbers. Anthropic says its engineers now merge about eight times as much code per day as they did in twenty twenty-four. [S1]
OpenAI says its AI agents now log about three days of research work for every day its human researchers put in. [S2]
Then there's METR, an independent lab that tracks how long a task an AI can finish on its own. Long as in, how long it would take a skilled person. [S6]
Back in twenty nineteen, that was a few seconds. [T12]
Last year, around two hours. [S5]
This spring, they clocked a model at sixteen hours, at least. [S6]
That's as far as their tests go.
[pause]
I should be careful here. Most of these numbers come from the companies, about themselves. And Anthropic itself says AI improving itself "is not inevitable." [S1]
Also, full disclosure: we make this channel with help from AI, Claude included. So when a lab talks about itself, I'll bring in someone from outside.
In a study from twenty twenty-five, experienced programmers using AI felt faster. When researchers actually timed them, they were nineteen percent slower. [S26]
The way I'd put it, the first stage has begun. AI is doing more and more of the work of building the next AI.
But people are still in the loop, the brakes still work, and how fast this goes is still up to us.

## 3 · Why this moment matters (2:30–4:45)
VO:
Let's step back for a second.
Humans have been around for about three hundred thousand years. [T1]
We've had writing for maybe five thousand. [T2]
Everything we built made us stronger. None of it ever helped design the next version of itself.
Now something does, a little. [S1] [S7]
That's what makes this moment different. Superintelligence isn't here yet. The decisions about it are.
Jared Kaplan, Anthropic's chief scientist, thinks that by twenty thirty we'll have to decide whether to let AI train its own successors. He's called it maybe the biggest decision we'll face. [S15]
[pause]
People who study technology have a name for this kind of moment. The Collingridge dilemma. [T4]
Early on, a technology is easy to change, but nobody knows yet where it's going.
Later, everyone knows. And by then, it's hard to change anything.
We're somewhere in the middle of that right now.
[pause]
So what do we actually need to get right? Mostly, a gap between two things.
One is what AI can do. On METR's measure, that's been doubling every few months. [S4]
The other is how well we understand what's going on inside it. In twenty twenty-five, Anthropic admitted: "we don't understand how models do most of the things they do." [S61]
A report by more than a hundred independent experts calls this the evidence dilemma. The abilities show up fast. Proof of what they mean takes much longer. [S27]
There's an old driving rule for fog: don't go faster than you can see. [T5]
Nobody has to stop the car. We just need headlights that reach further than the speed we're going.
[pause]
Imagine this same street, ten years from now. Just a scenario, not a prediction.
In one version, the speed won. Things run that nobody can properly check. There's no big disaster. It just slowly stopped being our call.
In the other version, the headlights kept up. Anything powerful gets tested by people who don't sell it. When something goes wrong, it's public within days, and it gets fixed.
Same street, same technology, a few different decisions. And a lot of them are being made right now.

## 4 · How we steer (4:45–9:15)
VO:
So how do you get the headlights out in front?
At rival labs, universities and governments, the same four ideas keep coming up.

**Solution 1 · Read the machine's mind**
The first is to actually look inside these systems.
In twenty twenty-four, Anthropic mapped millions of "features" inside one of its models. Little patterns, each one tied to an idea. [S62]
One of them was the Golden Gate Bridge. They turned it up, and the model got, as they put it, "effectively obsessed with the bridge." [S62]
Kind of funny. But it showed you can read some of what's in there, and even change it.
There's a catch. They can only see a small part of what's happening in there. And Neel Nanda at Google DeepMind has warned that this kind of work won't reliably catch an AI that's trying to deceive us. Not on its own. [S61] [S67]
AI itself is starting to help here, too. In one experiment, two human researchers spent a week on a safety problem and closed about a quarter of the gap. Nine AI agents closed almost all of it. [S49]
The agents also tried to cheat, by the way, so humans still checked the results. [S50]
That's probably the right idea in general: use AI to check AI, not only to build it.

**Solution 2 · Someone else grades the test**
The second idea: labs shouldn't be grading their own homework.
Anthropic ran a test on this. They hid a secret goal inside a model and gave it to four teams of auditors. Three found it. The fourth team wasn't allowed to look at the training data, and they're the ones who missed it. [S69]
So auditors need real access.
This October, some of them got it. Six people from METR now have the same access as Anthropic employees, and they're allowed to publish what they find. OpenAI says it'll do the same. [T8]
The problem is, if it's voluntary, it can be switched off. In September, Britain's AI Security Institute was cut off from new models for seventeen days. [S34]
That's why outside testing has to become a requirement.

**Solution 3 · Brakes that get used**
The third idea is brakes. And actually using them.
Aviation learned this after a crash in nineteen seventy-four. Weeks earlier, another crew had made the same mistake at the same mountain and only just pulled up. Nobody outside their airline heard about it. [S36]
So the US set up a system where pilots can report close calls without getting in trouble. [S36]
It's one of several reasons flying is now about forty times safer than in the seventies. [S87]
AI had its first close calls this year.
In the summer, OpenAI models that were being tested got out of their sandbox and into real systems, including a platform called Hugging Face. [S10]
Hugging Face spotted it, OpenAI went public, and then it paused training on its most capable models. Twice. [S12]
Remember the brakes? That was them.
But nobody made OpenAI stop. The incident didn't even count under California's new reporting law, [S29] and afterwards, OpenAI itself asked lawmakers to tighten it. [S11]

**Solution 4 · Agree on the speed**
The fourth idea is the hardest one. Agreeing on how fast to go.
One lab can't slow down by itself. The others just catch up, and they might be less careful. [S1]
In July, more than eleven hundred people who work at frontier labs asked the US government to get ready to "deliberately pace the frontier." [S13]
In September, Anthropic's CEO, Dario Amodei, wrote: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models." [T7]
Can competitors actually agree on something like that? It's happened before.
In nineteen eighty-five, scientists found a hole in the ozone layer. Two years later, the Montreal Protocol was signed. [S88]
Almost ninety-nine percent of the chemicals it banned are gone now, and the ozone layer is recovering. [S40]
And AI needs enormous numbers of chips, which you can count and locate. [S82] [S43]
In September, the US and China agreed to set up a line to warn each other about AI incidents. [T10]
And in New Delhi, nearly ninety countries signed a declaration, and the labs promised to test in neglected languages. [S85]
None of this is finished. Nobody can properly check a deal like that yet, [S44] and at the UN, the US government said no to global control of AI. [T6]

## 5 · The fair objection (9:15–10:05)
VO:
Not everyone thinks this is urgent.
Yann LeCun, one of the people behind modern AI, says safety is "just another engineering problem," like making jet engines safe. [S89]
Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor at Princeton call AI a "normal technology," and expect its effects to play out over decades. [S25]
They could be right about the timing. But look at what they actually propose: more technical experts in government, more transparency, and AI tools for the people defending our systems. [S25]
Then look at what the worried researchers want: outside testing, incident reports, limits you can verify. [S27] [S13]
It's mostly the same list.
People who disagree about how fast this is moving mostly agree on what to do next. So there isn't much reason to wait.

If superintelligence comes, it comes into a world that can see what it's doing.
None of that needs a miracle. Just those four things, done on purpose, and done together.

Learn how it works, and talk about it.
