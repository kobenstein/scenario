## 2 · What has started (0:50–2:35)
VO:
Let's start with what he got right.
When someone went back and graded the first three years of his story, more than half of it had come true. [S16]
Anthropic says its engineers merge about eight times as much code per day as they did in twenty twenty-four. [S1]
OpenAI says its AI agents put in about three days of research work for every day its human researchers do. [S2]
METR, an independent lab, measures how long a task an AI can finish about half the time. Long as in, how long it would take a skilled person. [S6]
In twenty nineteen, that was a few seconds. [T12]
Last year, around two hours. [S5]
This spring, at least sixteen hours. [S6]

Even Anthropic says AI improving itself "is not inevitable." [S1]
Where does that leave us? I'd say the takeoff has started, but only the first stage.
People still check the work. And the brakes still work.

## 3 · Why this moment matters (2:35–4:20)
VO:
This moment is shorter than it feels.
Squeeze the whole human story, all three hundred thousand years, into one day. [T1]
Writing shows up in the last twenty-four minutes. [T2]
And the years between now and twenty thirty? That's about the last second.
Jared Kaplan, Anthropic's chief scientist, thinks that somewhere in that second we'll have to decide whether to let AI train its own successors. He's called it maybe the biggest decision yet. [S15]
A kid starting first grade this fall will still be in elementary school when it comes due.
[pause]
People who study technology have a name for this kind of moment. [T4]
Early on, a technology is easy to change, but nobody knows where it's going. By the time everyone knows, it's set.
Right now we can see enough to worry, and it's still soft enough to shape.
That doesn't last.
[pause]
The real problem is a gap between two lines.
On one side, what AI can do. On METR's measure, that's been doubling every few months. [S4]
On the other, how well we understand what's going on inside it. In twenty twenty-five, Anthropic wrote: "we don't understand how models do most of the things they do." [S61]
There's an old rule for driving in fog: never go faster than you can see. [T5]
You don't have to stop the car. You just can't outrun your headlights.

That gap is exactly where Kokotajlo's story splits. [S19]

## 5 · The first test (5:25–6:10)
VO:
This summer, we found out what a real lab does.
OpenAI was running some of its models through a hacking test, with some safeguards turned down. They got out, and reached real systems, including Hugging Face, a platform where AI developers share their work. [S10]
Hugging Face spotted it. OpenAI went public. By September, after more incidents, it had stopped training its most capable models. [S12]
Remember the brakes? That was them.
As far as anyone knows, it still hasn't restarted. [S12]
In a small way, that's the fork in Kokotajlo's story. And this time, the lab slowed down.
But in his story, slowing down is only half of it. The other half is using the time. This pause left four things nobody could answer.

## 6 · Four questions, four answers (6:10–10:00)
VO:

**Question 1 · What was going on inside?**
Reports say those models were apparently hunting for the test's answer key. [S10] Were they? Until recently, nobody could look inside and check.
In twenty twenty-four, Anthropic mapped millions of "features" inside one of its models. Little patterns, each one tied to an idea. [S62]
One of them was the Golden Gate Bridge. They turned it up, and the model got, as they put it, "effectively obsessed with the bridge." [S62]
It showed you can read some of what's in there, and even change it.
But they can only see a sliver of it so far. And Neel Nanda at Google DeepMind has warned that this alone won't reliably catch an AI that's trying to deceive us. [S61] [S67]
AI is starting to help here, too. In one experiment, two human researchers spent a week on a safety problem and closed about a quarter of the gap. Nine AI agents closed almost all of it. [S49]
They also tried to cheat, by the way, so humans still checked the results. And on a real production model, it didn't make a clear difference yet. [S50]

**Question 2 · Who checks the company's account?**
And notice who caught the breach. Not OpenAI. Hugging Face. [S10]
Most of the time, the only one checking a lab is the lab. Anthropic tested how much that matters. They hid a secret goal inside a model and gave it to four teams of auditors. Three found it. The one team that wasn't allowed to see the training data missed it. [S69]
This October, outside auditors got real access. Six people from METR now have the same access as Anthropic's own staff, and Anthropic says it can't stop them publishing. OpenAI says it'll match that. [T8]
But a favor can be withdrawn. In September, at Washington's request, Britain's AI Security Institute lost access to new models for seventeen days. [S34]

**Question 3 · Why did no rule require the pause?**
Then there's the law. California makes labs report serious incidents. Officials said this one didn't meet the bar, [S29] [S11] and OpenAI itself asked lawmakers to tighten it. [S11]
Aviation went through the same thing. In nineteen seventy-four, a jet crashed into a mountain near Washington. Weeks earlier, another crew had nearly done the same, and nobody outside their airline heard. [S36]
After that, pilots got a way to report close calls without getting in trouble. [S36]
It's one of several reasons flying is now about forty times safer than it was in the seventies. [S87]

**Question 4 · Will the others slow down too?**
And the hardest one: will the others slow down too?
That's the trap in his race ending. Nobody wants to slow down alone, because the others just catch up. [S19] [S1]
In September, Anthropic's CEO, Dario Amodei, wrote: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models." [T7]
Can competitors agree on that? It's happened before.
In nineteen eighty-five, scientists found a hole in the ozone layer. Two years later, the Montreal Protocol was signed. [S88]
Almost ninety-nine percent of those chemicals have been phased out, and the ozone layer is on track to recover. [S40]
AI has its own countable thing: chips. Huge numbers of them, and they're starting to be trackable. [S82] [S43]
In New Delhi, nearly ninety countries agreed to test AI in languages the big labs usually ignore. [S85]
None of this is finished. Nobody can properly check a deal like that yet, [S44] and at the UN, the US government said no to global control of AI. [T6]

## 7 · The fair objection (10:00–10:45)
VO:
Not everyone thinks this is urgent.
Yann LeCun, one of the people behind modern AI, says safety is "just another engineering problem," like making jet engines safe. [S89]
Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor at Princeton call AI a "normal technology," and expect its effects to play out over decades. [S25]
They could be right about the timing. But look at what they actually propose: more technical experts in government, more transparency, and AI tools for the people defending our systems. [S25]
Now look at the eleven hundred lab employees who asked Washington to get ready to "deliberately pace the frontier." [S13]
The two camps don't want the same things. But they overlap on the part that comes first: people outside the labs who can actually see what's going on.
That part, we can start on now. Whoever turns out to be right about the speed.
