## 3 · Why this moment matters (2:35–4:20)
VO:
Let's step back for a second.
Humans have been around for about three hundred thousand years. [T1]
We've had writing for maybe five thousand. [T2]
Everything we built made us stronger. None of it ever helped design the next version of itself.
That's what makes this moment different. Superintelligence isn't here yet. The decisions about it are.
Jared Kaplan, Anthropic's chief scientist, thinks that by twenty thirty we'll have to decide whether to let AI train its own successors. He's called it maybe the biggest decision we'll face. [S15]
People who study technology have a name for this kind of moment, the Collingridge dilemma. [T4]
Early on, a technology is easy to change, but nobody knows where it's going. Later, everyone knows, and by then it's hard to change anything.
We're somewhere in the middle.
[pause]
What we have to get right is mostly a gap.
On one side, what AI can do. On METR's measure, that's been doubling every few months. [S4]
On the other, how well we understand what's going on inside it. In twenty twenty-five, Anthropic admitted: "we don't understand how models do most of the things they do." [S61]
There's an old driving rule for fog: don't go faster than you can see. [T5]
Nobody has to stop the car. We need headlights that reach further than the speed we're going.

## 6 · Four questions, four answers (6:10–10:00)
VO:

**Question 1 · What was going on inside?**
The first question: what was the model actually thinking?
For a long time, nobody could answer that. In twenty twenty-four, Anthropic mapped millions of "features" inside one of its models. Little patterns, each one tied to an idea. [S62]
One of them was the Golden Gate Bridge. They turned it up, and the model got, as they put it, "effectively obsessed with the bridge." [S62]
Kind of funny. But it showed you can read some of what's in there, and even change it.
There's a catch. They can only see a small part of it so far. And Neel Nanda at Google DeepMind has warned that this alone won't reliably catch an AI that's trying to deceive us. [S61] [S67]
AI is starting to help here, too. In one experiment, two human researchers spent a week on a safety problem and closed about a quarter of the gap. Nine AI agents closed almost all of it. [S49]
They also tried to cheat, by the way, so humans still checked the results. [S50]

**Question 2 · Who checks the company's account?**
The second question: who checks what the company tells us?
Right now, mostly the company. Anthropic tested how much that matters. They hid a secret goal inside a model and gave it to four teams of auditors. Three found it. The one team that wasn't allowed to see the training data missed it. [S69]
This October, outside auditors got real access. Six people from METR now work inside Anthropic like employees, and they're allowed to publish what they find. OpenAI says it'll do the same. [T8]
But anything voluntary can be switched off. In September, Britain's AI Security Institute was cut off from new models for seventeen days. [S34]
That's why outside testing needs to become a requirement.

**Question 3 · Why did no rule require the pause?**
The third question: why did no rule require that pause?
California has a new law that makes labs report serious incidents. This one didn't count. [S29] Afterwards, OpenAI itself asked lawmakers to tighten it. [S11]
Aviation went through the same thing. In nineteen seventy-four, a passenger jet crashed into a mountain near Washington. Weeks earlier, another crew had made the same mistake there and only just pulled up. Nobody outside their airline heard about it. [S36]
After that, the US set up a system where pilots can report close calls without getting in trouble. [S36]
It's one of several reasons flying is now about forty times safer than it was in the seventies. [S87]

**Question 4 · Will the others slow down too?**
And the hardest one: will the others slow down too?
One lab can't do it alone. The others just catch up, and they might be less careful. [S1]
In July, more than eleven hundred people who work at frontier labs asked the US government to get ready to "deliberately pace the frontier." [S13]
In September, Anthropic's CEO, Dario Amodei, wrote: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models." [T7]
Can competitors agree on something like that? It's happened before.
In nineteen eighty-five, scientists found a hole in the ozone layer. Two years later, the Montreal Protocol was signed. [S88]
Almost ninety-nine percent of the chemicals it banned are gone now, and the ozone layer is recovering. [S40]
And AI needs huge numbers of chips, which you can count and locate. [S82] [S43]
In September, the US and China agreed to set up a line to warn each other about AI incidents. [T10]
In New Delhi, nearly ninety countries agreed to test AI in languages the big labs usually ignore. [S85]
None of this is finished. Nobody can properly check a deal like that yet, [S44] and at the UN, the US government said no to global control of AI. [T6]

## 7 · The fair objection (10:00–10:45)
VO:
Not everyone thinks this is urgent.
Yann LeCun, one of the people behind modern AI, says safety is "just another engineering problem," like making jet engines safe. [S89]
Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor at Princeton call AI a "normal technology," and expect its effects to play out over decades. [S25]
They could be right about the timing. But look at what they actually propose: more technical experts in government, more transparency, and AI tools for the people defending our systems. [S25]
Then look at what the worried researchers want: outside testing, incident reports, limits you can verify. [S27] [S13]
It's mostly the same list.
People who disagree about how fast this is moving mostly agree on what to do next. So there isn't much reason to wait.

## 8 · Where it can lead, and your part (10:45–12:30)
