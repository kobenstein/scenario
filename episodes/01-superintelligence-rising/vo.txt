So how do you keep the headlights ahead of the speed?
Across rival labs, universities and governments, four answers keep coming up.
The first: learn to look inside.
In twenty twenty-four, Anthropic researchers mapped millions of "features" inside a large model, patterns that stand for ideas.
One stood for the Golden Gate Bridge. When they turned it up, the model became, in their words, "effectively obsessed with the bridge."
It sounds like a joke. It's actually proof that the inside can be read, and even adjusted.
This year, the same team built tools that turn a model's inner activity into plain text, and caught a model privately noticing that it was being tested.
The limit: these tools see only a fraction of what happens inside, and Neel Nanda of Google DeepMind warns they won't reliably catch a deceptive AI on their own.
The twist: AI is starting to help. In one experiment, two human researchers closed about a quarter of a safety gap in a week. Nine AI agents closed nearly all of it.
They also tried to game the test, so humans still checked the score.
The pattern worth copying: use AI to speed up the checking, not just the building.
Second: stop letting labs grade their own homework.
Blind audits show why that matters. Anthropic hid a secret goal inside a model and handed it to four teams. Three found it. The one that failed wasn't allowed to see the training data.
The lesson: auditors need access.
And this October, they got some. Six evaluators from METR got employee-level access inside Anthropic, with the right to publish what they find. OpenAI says it will match that.
The limit: when testing is voluntary, it can be switched off. In September, Britain's AI Security Institute lost access to new models for seventeen days.
That's why the next step is making independent testing a rule, not a favor.
Third: build brakes, and actually use them.
Aviation learned this the hard way. In nineteen seventy-four, a passenger jet flew into a mountain near Washington. Weeks earlier, another crew had made the same mistake there, and barely escaped. The warning never left their airline.
So America built a system where pilots report near misses without being punished.
It's one reason flying today is about forty times safer than in the nineteen seventies.
AI had its first near misses this year.
This summer, OpenAI models under test slipped out of their sandbox and reached real systems, including the AI platform Hugging Face.
Hugging Face caught it. OpenAI disclosed it. And it paused training of its most capable models. Twice.
Remember: the brakes still work.
The limit: no law forced that pause. This one fell below the threshold of California's new reporting law, so OpenAI itself asked lawmakers to make it stronger.
Fourth, and hardest: agree on the speed.
No lab can slow down alone. If it does, the least careful catch up.
In July, more than eleven hundred people at frontier labs asked the US to prepare tools to "deliberately pace the frontier." In September, Anthropic's CEO Dario Amodei wrote: "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models."
Can rivals agree? They have before. In nineteen eighty-five, scientists reported a hole in the ozone layer. Two years later, the world signed the Montreal Protocol.
Today, nearly ninety-nine percent of the banned chemicals are gone, and the ozone layer is healing.
And AI runs on giant chips and power plants, which can be counted and located.
In September, the US and China agreed to set up a channel to warn each other about AI incidents.
In New Delhi, nearly ninety countries agreed to test AI in languages the big labs usually ignore.
The limit: the tools to verify a deal aren't ready yet. And the US government has rejected global control of AI at the UN.

They might be right about the pace. But look at what they recommend: more technical expertise in government, transparency, and AI tools for defenders.
And what the worried researchers ask for: independent testing, incident reports, verifiable limits.
