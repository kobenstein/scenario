# 04 · 1 Billion AI Agents Want Our Jobs. What Should We Do?
Insight: Whether AI agents make us richer or poorer is not decided by the technology, but by who owns them and the rules we write now.
Misconception: The question is "Is my job safe?" (a technology question). It is a question of ownership and rules.
Through-line image: the farmer and the horse (1900, the tractor arrives). Harvest: "Horses couldn't own anything. They couldn't vote. We can do both."
Status: DRAFT 1 (2026-10-19). [S#] = sources.md. CLIP = real interview audio/video with timestamp.

## 1 · Hook (0:00–0:35)
VO:
Somewhere right now, a new kind of worker is starting its first day.
It doesn't sleep. It doesn't take lunch. It never asks for a raise.
And soon, there could be a billion of them.
[pause]
Analysts at IDC expect more than a billion AI agents at work by twenty twenty-nine. [S1]
Not robots. Small programs that take a task and finish it on their own.
They answer the customer. They write the code. They book the shipment.
[pause]
Most people ask one question about this. Is my job safe?
It's a fair question. But it's not the one that decides how this ends.
That one is simpler. When the agents do the work, who gets paid?

## 3 · The canary (1:35–2:30)
VO:
A hundred years ago, miners carried a canary underground.
If the air turned bad, the bird felt it first.
Today, the canary is twenty-two years old.
At Stanford, Erik Brynjolfsson's team followed the payroll records of millions of American workers. [S7]
In the jobs most exposed to AI, like software and customer service, workers aged twenty-two to twenty-five are now about nineteen percent behind where they would have been. [S7]
Older workers in the same jobs? Doing fine.
Mostly, nobody fired the young ones. They just weren't hired.
[pause]
And here's the strange part. AI makes beginners better.
In a study of more than five thousand support agents, an AI assistant made the newest workers about a third more productive. [S8]
So why hire fewer of them?
Because a company can use the same tool in two ways.
To make a beginner as good as a veteran. Or to need fewer beginners at all.
That's not something the technology decides. Someone chooses it.
Keep that in mind.

A ladder of light: the bottom rung thins and flickers. Then a split: one cyan ring rises beside the beginner and lifts her ("better"); on the other side the same ring takes her chair ("fewer").

## 4 · Who gets paid (2:30–3:20)

## 5 · Farmers and horses (3:20–4:40)
VO:
We've lived through a machine like this before.
In nineteen hundred, about four in ten Americans worked on farms. [S11]
And beside them worked more than twenty million horses. [S12]
Then came the tractor.
The farmers' children found new work, in factories and offices. Today fewer than two in a hundred Americans farm. [S11]
The horses found nothing. Within sixty years, most of them were gone. [S12]
[pause]
So here's the question for this century. Are we the farmers, or the horses?
[pause]
Many economists say we're the farmers. [S6]
Two hundred years of machines, and work never ran out. When things get cheaper, we want more of them. And ageing countries will need more hands, not fewer.
They could be right.
But last time, people escaped from muscle work into thinking work.
This time, the machine is learning the thinking too. And it learns the new jobs as fast as we invent them.
[pause]
Here's the part that gives me hope.
The horses didn't lose because they were slow. They lost because they couldn't own the tractor, and they couldn't vote on the rules.
We can do both.

## 7 · Two endings (5:15–6:30)
VO:
Let's imagine it's twenty thirty-two. This is a scenario, not a forecast.
Your little sister has just finished her degree.
[pause]
In the first future, the agents belong to a handful of companies.
Her inbox fills with polite rejections. An agent wrote every one.
Many things got cheaper. Rent didn't.
The country is richer than ever. Most people in it aren't.
Nothing collapsed. We just let the gains flow to whoever owned the machines.
[pause]
In the second future, it's the same desk. The same laptop.
She starts as a junior who runs a small team of agents, with a senior who trains her.
Every autumn, a dividend lands in her account. Her share of the machines, like every citizen's.
And when her father's job was automated, the cushion was there before the fall.
Same technology. Different rules.

## 8 · How we get there (6:30–9:40)
VO:
I don't know which future we'll get. Nobody does.
But the second one stands on three layers. None of them is magic. Each one already works somewhere.
[pause]
The first layer: keep the bottom rung.
Remember the choice: make beginners better, or need fewer of them.
Right now, the rules tilt that choice.
In America, a company pays roughly thirty percent in taxes on a worker, and around five percent on the software and machines that could replace one. [S13]
We're subsidising our own replacement.
Taxing both the same isn't punishing technology. It's a level field.
And some companies choose the other path.
IBM says it's tripling its entry-level hiring this year, and rewriting those jobs so juniors check the AI's work and spend more time with customers. [S14]
In Switzerland, about two in three teenagers learn a trade inside a real company. [S15]
Experience is the one thing you can't download.
And yes, some people will lead the agents instead of competing with them.
A tiny team in Israel built an app business with AI and sold it for eighty million dollars. [S16]
But that's a path for thousands, not millions.
And even there, by early twenty twenty-six, almost all of their revenue went straight to the AI companies they ran on. [S16]
Which brings us to the money.
[pause]
The second layer: own a piece of the machines.
In the nineteen seventies, oil money poured into Alaska. Its governor, Jay Hammond, had an idea. The oil belongs to everyone, so everyone gets a share. [S17]
Since nineteen eighty-two, every resident gets a check from that fund. This year, twelve hundred dollars. [S17]
People didn't stop working. Poverty fell. [S17]
And this year, the idea jumped to AI.
OpenAI has proposed a public wealth fund that would give every citizen a stake. [S18]
Senator Bernie Sanders wants half of the biggest AI companies in a citizens' fund. [S19]
American babies born from twenty twenty-five to twenty twenty-eight can get a thousand dollars in an investment account. [S20]
Right now it may only hold index funds. Anthropic, itself an AI company, has suggested letting it hold a stake in AI companies too. [S20]
But Alaska also shows the catch.
Since twenty sixteen, politicians have cut the check again and again to fill budget gaps. [S21]
A share only protects you if the rule is written down before the money arrives.
[pause]
The third layer: a floor that rises with the need.
Sam Altman paid for one of the largest cash experiments ever. A thousand dollars a month, for three years, no strings. [S22]
The results were honest, and mixed.
People worked a little less, about an hour or two a week, and mostly gained free time.
Their stress dropped, but by the second year the relief had faded. [S22]
Money takes away fear. It doesn't give you a reason to get up in the morning.
In the early nineteen thirties, in the Austrian village of Marienthal, the psychologist Marie Jahoda studied what unemployment does to people. [S23]
They lost their sense of time. They stopped meeting each other.
In twenty twenty, the same village tried the opposite. A guaranteed job for everyone out of work for a long time. [S23]
People got back their structure, their friends, the feeling of being needed. And it cost about the same as unemployment. [S23]
In Washington, one bill would connect the pieces: a tax on AI that rises automatically when unemployment rises, and pays for jobs in care and housing. [S24]
It's only a proposal. But it works like a thermostat. The answer grows with the problem.
[pause]
None of these layers builds itself.
AI companies, governments, and the voters who choose those governments are writing these rules right now.

## 9 · Your part (9:40–10:30)
VO:
So what can you do?
Use the agents to learn, not to skip learning. Ask them why, not just what.
If you hire people, ask whether AI makes your juniors better, or just makes them unnecessary. Then hire the junior.
Don't bargain alone. In twenty twenty-three, Hollywood's writers bargained together, and won a rule that AI can't write the scripts that replace them. [S25]
And as a citizen, ask the question that decides all of this. Who will own the agents?
Ask it now, while the rules are still being written.
[pause]
A billion agents don't want anything.
The horses couldn't own anything. They couldn't vote.
We can do both.
[pause]
So here's my question for you.
If you owned a share of every AI agent's work, what would you do with your time?
